# Evidence-Based Visual, Character, Animation and Audio Design for a Voice-First AI Character (Children Aged 6–8)

Scope note: The product is a laptop web app (sometimes mirrored to a large screen). One child aged 6–8 talks with an animated cartoon face for about 5 minutes while a facilitator adult runs it. Evidence strength tags used below:
- **[Strong]**: replicated or meta-analytic, with child samples.
- **[Moderate]**: a single good study with children, or several studies with adjacent ages.
- **[Weak/Adult]**: adult-only data, secondary summaries, or practitioner sources extrapolated to ages 6–8.
- "(not fetched)" means the citation was given from known bibliographic details and was not re-opened in this session.

---

## 1. Character form: baby schema, round vs angular shapes, large eyes, friendliness and trust, and the uncanny valley (cartoon vs realistic)

### Takeaway
Children of 6–8 already respond to baby-schema features: a round face, high forehead, large eyes, and small nose and mouth. Six- and eight-year-olds (but not 4-year-olds) prefer baby-featured teddy bears. Curved forms read as safe or pleasant and angular or downward-pointing forms as threatening. Children under about 9 do not yet show the uncanny valley, but adults (facilitators, parents) do. Schematic or cartoon faces are also easier for children to read emotionally. A stylised, rounded cartoon face is therefore the evidence-favoured choice over a realistic human avatar.

### Cited Findings
- **Baby-schema definition.** Kindchenschema means a large head, a round face, a high and protruding forehead, large eyes, and a small nose and mouth. — [Borgi et al. 2014, *Frontiers in Psychology* 5:411](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00411)
- **Children respond to baby schema [Moderate].** Borgi et al. parametrically manipulated baby schema in human, dog and cat faces. Children aged 3–6 gave higher cuteness ratings to high-baby-schema faces, and eye-tracking showed their gaze went to those infantile stimuli and to specific facial features. The authors conclude that the response "emerges early during development". Children rated adult dog faces cuter than adult cat or adult human faces. — [Borgi et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00411); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/07/140721100119.htm)
- **Teddy-bear preference is age-dependent [Moderate].** Children aged 4, 6 and 8 chose among eight teddies, four with adult features and four with infantile features. The 6- and 8-year-olds preferred the baby-featured bears; the 4-year-olds did not. The authors suggest nurturant preference develops relatively late. This places the target age (6–8) squarely in the group that prefers babyish features. — [Morris, Reddy & Bunting 1995, *Animal Behaviour* 50:1697–1700](https://researchportal.port.ac.uk/en/publications/the-survival-of-the-cutest-whos-responsible-for-the-evolution-of-/)
- **Baby schema raises cuteness and caretaking motivation in adults [Moderate, adult].** Real infant photos were morphed to high baby schema (round face, high forehead, large eyes, small nose and mouth) or low baby schema (narrow face, low forehead, small eyes, large nose and mouth). The n = 122 undergraduates rated high-baby-schema faces cuter and showed stronger caretaking motivation. — [Glocker et al. 2009, *Ethology* 115(3):257–263, DOI 10.1111/j.1439-0310.2008.01603.x](https://www.cceb.upenn.edu/csa/assets/user-content/documents/BabySchemainInfantFacesInducesCutenessPerceptionandMotivationforCaretakinginAdults.pdf)
- **Neural reward response [Moderate, adult].** A follow-up fMRI study found a linear increase in nucleus-accumbens (reward) activity with increasing baby schema. — [Glocker et al. 2009, *PNAS*](https://www.cceb.upenn.edu/csa/assets/user-content/documents/PNAS-2009-Glocker.pdf)
- **Curved objects are preferred [Moderate, adult].** Adults prefer curved over sharp-angled versions of the same objects. The authors attribute this to sharp contours implying threat. — [Bar & Neta 2006, *Psychological Science* 17(8):645–648](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01759.x) (not fetched)
- **Shapes carry implicit affect [Moderate, adult].** Two IAT experiments showed that simple shapes carry affective value without explicit judgment: downward-pointing triangles were categorised as "unpleasant" faster. Angular patterns are associated with threat and curvilinear forms with pleasantness. — [Larson, Aronoff & Steuer 2012, *Motivation & Emotion* 36:404–413](https://scholarhub.vn/publication/Simple-geometric-shapes-are-implicitly-associated-with-affective-value/73d73b5c-9b7e-43ef-9f24-988957641bc7)
- **Downward V-shapes activate threat circuitry [Moderate, adult].** Simple forms made only of downward-pointing V-shapes activate the amygdala and other threat-detection regions more than other forms. — [Larson et al. 2009, *J. Cognitive Neuroscience*](https://einstein.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/recognizing-threat-a-simple-geometric-shape-activates-neural-circ/)
  - Implication: a "V" brow shape is the classic angry-brow cue.
- **Sound–shape mapping is present early [Moderate].** Children as young as about 2.5 years map round shapes to "bouba"-type sounds and spiky shapes to "kiki"-type sounds. — [Maurer, Pathman & Mondloch 2006, *Developmental Science* 9(3):316–322](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-7687.2006.00495.x) (not fetched)
- **The uncanny valley is acquired in childhood [Moderate].** Children aged 3–18 (about 240 in total, per secondary summary) watched videos of machine-like and human-like robots. Children under about 9 did not report uncanny or "creepy" feelings. Older children, like adults, found the very human-like robot creepier. — [Brink, Gray & Wellman 2019, *Child Development* 90(4):1202–1214, author manuscript](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/handle/2027.42/150519/cdev12999_am.pdf); [NPR summary](https://www.npr.org/sections/13.7/2017/12/16/563075762/hi-robot-adults-children-and-the-uncanny-valley)
- **Appearance drives perceived intent [Moderate].** In a survey, 159 children rated 40 robot images. Their judgments of friendly vs unfriendly intent and of emotional expression varied clearly with appearance. Per a secondary summary, children judged human-like robots as aggressive and mixed human-machine robots as friendly. The author read this as tentative support for the uncanny valley. — [Woods 2006, *Interacting with Computers* 18(6):1390–1418](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/handle/2299/5383)
- **Schematic drawings are easier to read than photographs [Moderate].** For 138 preschoolers, stimulus type mattered significantly: children were more accurate identifying emotions in schematic drawings than in photographs (accuracy correlation across formats r = .677). — [Macdonald et al. 1996, PubMed 8976595](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8976595/)
  - A related dissertation with children aged 4, 5 and 8 plus adults found the same drawing advantage, with accuracy rising with age. — [ProQuest abstract](https://www.proquest.com/docview/304296946/abstract/A6A1045AB4324975PQ/1)
- **Children may rely on simplified features [Weak].** Children may rely more on low-level, simplified features of the kind found in cartoons to process facial emotion. — [Kendall et al. 2016, *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41235-016-0021-8)

### Inferences
- A stylised 2D or soft-3D cartoon face is the evidence-favoured choice for three reasons. Children of 6–8 like baby-schema features (Morris 1995; Borgi 2014). Schematic faces are easier for them to read emotionally (Macdonald 1996). And a realistic human avatar gains nothing for under-9s while risking creepiness for adults and children near 9 (Brink 2019). A mixed "almost-human" look is the worst option (Woods 2006).
- Proportions to aim for:
  - head- or face-dominant composition (the face fills most of the canvas);
  - eyes placed at or slightly below the vertical midline of the head, giving a high forehead;
  - each eye roughly 18–25% of face width, with large dark pupils and a white specular highlight;
  - small nose (or none);
  - mouth narrow at rest that opens wide when speaking;
  - full, round cheeks.
  - These numeric ratios are design extrapolations from the qualitative baby-schema features above. No study gives an optimal eye-to-face ratio for children.
- Use circles and superellipses for the silhouette, eyes and mouth. Avoid V-shaped brows, pointed ears or horns, and sharp teeth (Bar & Neta 2006; Larson et al. 2009/2012). Express "surprised" or "curious" with raised, arched brows rather than angled ones.
- A non-human or creature-like character (a friendly animal or blob) sidesteps human-likeness concerns and benefits from children rating animal faces as cute (Borgi 2014). This is an inference: the dog-face result was for photographs, not cartoons.

### Gaps
- No study found that gives quantitative eye-size or face-proportion optima for cartoon characters for children aged 6–8.
- Fullard & Reiling (1976, *Child Development*), often cited as showing that children aged 7–12 prefer adult over infant faces, was not verified in this session. If accurate, it conflicts in part with Borgi (2014) and Morris (1995); it likely differs because it measured preference for photos of infants rather than cuteness or toy choice.
- No child-specific evidence was found for bouba/kiki mapping onto character *trustworthiness*. That link rests on adult data plus the toddler sound–shape study.

## 2. Colour preferences, emotional associations, and accessibility

### Takeaway
At ages 5–6.5, children associate bright colours mostly with positive emotions and dark colours (black, brown, grey) with negative ones. About 69% of their colour–emotion associations are positive. Red–green colour blindness affects about 1 in 12 boys of Northern European descent, so state and UI meaning must never rely on hue alone. Use CVD-safe accents (Okabe–Ito), strong luminance contrast, and redundant shape or motion cues.

### Cited Findings
- **Children's colour–emotion associations [Moderate].** Sixty children (30 girls, 30 boys) in two age groups, 5 and 6.5 years, were shown nine colours. Overall, 69% of their colour–emotion associations were positive. Bright colours (pink, red, yellow, green, purple, blue) drew more positive responses than dark colours (black, brown, grey). Positive emotions were coded as happiness, strength and excitement; negative as sadness, anger and boredom. Boys were more likely than girls to feel positive about dark colours. — [Boyatzis & Varghese 1994, *Journal of Genetic Psychology* 155(1):77–85](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8021626/); [full-text PDF](https://moodle2.units.it/pluginfile.php/413780/mod_resource/content/0/Verghese1994.pdf)
- **Brightness and saturation in adults [Weak/Adult].** In adults, brightness is strongly and positively related to pleasantness, and saturation is related mainly to arousal. — [Valdez & Mehrabian 1994, *JEP: General* 123(4):394–409](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.123.4.394) (not fetched)
- **Red–green colour blindness prevalence.** Up to 8% of men and 0.5% of women of Northern European ancestry have the common red–green colour blindness (NEI estimate). It is X-linked recessive, hence the sex gap. — [Smithsonian, citing the National Eye Institute](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/ask-smithsonian-how-does-world-look-the-color-blind-180960415/); [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/health/genetics/why-is-color-blindness-so-much-more-common-in-men-than-in-women)
  - Broader ranges are also quoted: 5–8% of men and 0.5–1% of women. — [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/health/Color-Blindness-Prevalence.aspx)
- **CVD-safe palette.** The Okabe–Ito "Color Universal Design" palette is distinguishable under the common colour-vision deficiencies:
  - black #000000, orange #E69F00, sky blue #56B4E9, bluish green #009E73
  - yellow #F0E442, blue #0072B2, vermillion #D55E00, reddish purple #CC79A7
  - Sources: [Okabe & Ito 2008, J*FLY](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/); [Wong 2011, *Nature Methods* 8:441](https://doi.org/10.1038/nmeth.1618) (not fetched)
- **WCAG contrast thresholds.** WCAG 2.1 requires at least 4.5:1 contrast for normal text, 3:1 for large text, and 3:1 for meaningful non-text graphics such as state indicators and icons. — [W3C WCAG 2.1, SC 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG21/#contrast-minimum) and [SC 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG21/#non-text-contrast) (not fetched)

### Inferences
- Build the character and scene from bright, warm, medium-to-high-saturation hues (apricot, coral, sunny yellow, mint or teal, sky blue) on a light warm background. Keep black, brown and grey to small details such as pupils and outlines (Boyatzis & Varghese 1994).
- Use dark navy (#1F2A44) rather than pure black for pupils, mouth interior and outlines. It keeps very high contrast (13.5:1 on cream) and is less harsh. This is a stylistic inference.
- Because roughly 1 in 12 boys may be red–green colour-blind, the listening, thinking and speaking states must differ in luminance, motion and icon shape as well as hue. Never use red vs green alone for "wrong/right" or "stop/go".
- Okabe orange and sky blue fail 3:1 on a light background. Use them as fills with a navy outline, or use the darker Okabe blue and vermillion for thin indicator rings.

### Gaps
- No verified data was found in this session on children's saturation preferences. Child, Hansen & Hornbeck (1968, *Child Development* 39:237–247) is often cited for age trends: younger children preferring saturated and warm colours, and a reported shift toward cool hues. The abstract could not be confirmed.
- The per-colour percentages in Boyatzis & Varghese are in a table that was garbled in extraction. Only the overall 69% figure is verified.

## 3. Children's recognition of facial emotion (cartoon vs real), exaggeration, and eye gaze and contact

### Takeaway
By 6, children recognise happiness, sadness and anger almost as well as teenagers. Fear, surprise and disgust keep improving through childhood. Happiness is easiest. Children read schematic or cartoon faces more accurately than photographs. Gaze matters too: children treat the more nonverbally contingent robot as the better informant. Gaze aversion is read as "thinking" (in agents) and is used by children themselves by about 8.

### Cited Findings
- **Which emotions children read reliably [Moderate].** Happiness, sadness and anger were recognised almost as well by 6-year-olds as by 16-year-olds. Recognition of surprise, fear and disgust (and happiness, at ceiling) improved linearly with age, while sad and angry showed little change from 6 to 16.
  - Summarised in [Frontiers for Young Minds 2016](https://kids.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/frym.2016.00015).
  - Consistent with [Lawrence, Campbell & Skuse 2015, *Frontiers in Psychology* 6:761](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00761).
  - Exact figures were not verified in this session.
- **Ranking within ages 6–11 [Moderate].** Happiness was the most accurately identified expression, followed by disgust and surprise. The 6–7-year-old group had the lowest overall accuracy and the 10–11 group the highest. — [Guarnera et al. 2015, *Europe's Journal of Psychology*, PMC4873105](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4873105/)
- **Drawings beat photographs.** Schematic drawings yield higher emotion-recognition accuracy than photographs for preschoolers, and in a second sample spanning ages 4–8. — [Macdonald et al. 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8976595/); [ProQuest dissertation abstract](https://www.proquest.com/docview/304296946/abstract/A6A1045AB4324975PQ/1)
- **Children's own gaze aversion while thinking [Moderate].**
  - Older children, but not 5-year-olds, looked away from an interlocutor's face more for hard questions than easy ones. — [Doherty-Sneddon et al. 2002, *Developmental Psychology* 38(3)](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/development-of-gaze-aversion-as-disengagement-from-visual-informa/)
  - Gaze aversion use "increases dramatically" in the first school years and reaches adult levels by 8. Five-year-olds made quick multidirectional glances; 8-year-olds made sustained, mostly rightward shifts. — [Doherty-Sneddon & Phelps 2007, *British Journal of Developmental Psychology* 25(4)](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/development-of-gaze-aversion-qualitative-changes-over-the-early-s/)
  - Five-year-olds trained to look away while thinking answered hard questions significantly better than untrained controls. — [Phelps, Doherty-Sneddon & Warnock 2006, *BJDP* 24(3)](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/helping-children-think-gaze-aversion-and-teaching/)
- **Agent gaze aversion [Moderate, adult].** Gaze-aversion timings were modelled on human dyads. Virtual agents that averted gaze were perceived as thinking, elicited more disclosure, and regulated turn-taking better. Agents that averted gaze during a pause were yielded the floor more than agents that held steady eye contact or did not avert. — [Andrist, Mutlu & Gleicher 2013, IVA, pp. 249–262](https://peopleandrobots.wisc.edu/publications/conversational-gaze-aversion-for-virtual-agents-inproceedings/)
- **Nonverbal contingency drives trust [Moderate].** Children aged 3–5 met two robots giving information about unfamiliar animals. They attended most to, and were most receptive to, the robot that showed greater nonverbal contingency (responsive gaze and posture). Children treated robots as informants they could question and learn from. — [Breazeal et al. 2016, *Topics in Cognitive Science* 8(2):481–491](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/young-children-treat-robots-as-informants)

### Inferences
- **Expression set.** Limit the core set to expressions children of 6–8 read reliably and that suit a friendly buddy:
  - happy/joyful;
  - calm/attentive (listening);
  - curious/thinking;
  - sad/sympathetic (used sparingly and softly);
  - excited/celebrating;
  - gentle surprise ("oh!").
- Avoid anger, fear and disgust. They are less reliably read at 6–7 and inappropriate for the role.
- **Exaggeration.** Express each emotion with at least three redundant channels: brow shape, eye shape (squint or "smile eyes", widened, lids lowered) and mouth shape. Add whole-head motion, such as a bounce for joy or a tilt for curiosity. This follows the cartoon and schematic advantage and the animation principle of exaggeration (Section 5).
- **Gaze plan.**
  - Hold "eye contact" (pupils aimed at the camera or the child's detected face) while listening and at the start and end of speaking turns.
  - Avert gaze (up or to the side) during "thinking". It signals cognition (Andrist 2013) and matches what 8-year-olds do themselves (Doherty-Sneddon 2007).
  - Use the webcam to make gaze and head orientation *contingent* on the child, e.g. eyes follow the child's face position. Contingency drives attention and trust (Breazeal 2016).

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly measures 6–8-year-olds' recognition of *animated cartoon* (vs static schematic) expressions, or how much exaggeration is optimal.
- Andrist et al. 2013 used adult participants; child responses to agent gaze aversion are extrapolated.

## 4. Children's software UX: reading and text size, feedback and waiting, frustrations, and voice turn-taking cues

### Takeaway
NN/g distinguishes 3–5, 6–8 and 9–12 as distinct design audiences. Its findings: children want instant gratification, cannot interpret error messages, skip long text, like animation and sound, and expect characters to respond. Text should be at least 14 pt for young children, and beginning readers (5–7) read faster with larger text. For voice:
- Pre-schoolers repair failed voice turns mainly by repeating (79%) and talking louder (63%).
- ASR historically failed badly on child speech.
- Children take longer turn gaps than adults.
- Response-time studies suggest replying in about 1 s, at most about 2 s, with fillers masking longer delays.

### Cited Findings
- **NN/g research base and age bands.** The NN/g children's research rests on 125 children (59 girls, 66 boys) across studies in 2001, 2010 and 2018. Guidelines grew from 130 to 156. Designers must distinguish at minimum ages 3–5, 6–8 and 9–12. Children are "acutely aware of age differences" and react negatively to content one grade above or below their level. — [Sherwin & Nielsen 2019, NN/g, "Children's UX: Usability Issues in Designing for Young People"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/childrens-websites-usability-issues/)
- **NN/g font sizes and reading.**
  - Font size 14 pt for young children and 12 pt for older children.
  - The youngest children do not read; young children read tentatively; older children scan.
  - Children skipped lengthy instructions; avoid scrolling for young children.
  - Source: [Sherwin & Nielsen 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/childrens-websites-usability-issues/)
- **NN/g on feedback, animation and sound.**
  - Children "want instant gratification" and get frustrated by slow designs.
  - They like animation and sound, whereas adults usually dislike them.
  - They expect to tap characters and expect things to animate and play sound. Some tried tapping non-touch laptop screens and were disappointed.
  - They shrug off broken features and cannot interpret error messages.
  - Until about age 9 they prefer touchscreens and trackpads to mouse and keyboard.
  - Source: [Sherwin & Nielsen 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/childrens-websites-usability-issues/)
- **Text size for beginning readers [Moderate].** In a study of 120 children, reading speed of 5–7-year-olds fell as text size and spacing decreased. Children aged 8–11 were unaffected, and accuracy suffered on smaller text at all ages. Some beginning readers may benefit from larger text than current practice. — [Hughes & Wilkins 2000, *Journal of Research in Reading*, DOI 10.1111/1467-9817.00126](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/1467-9817.00126)
  - A 2002 "big books" study by the same authors found smaller x-height with larger spacing read equal or better, so the optimum is unsettled. — summarised in [PMC5934461](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5934461)
- **Legibility in visual angle [Weak, adult].** Character height subtending 20–22 arcmin generally assures legibility, and 16–18 arcmin may be acceptable with good contrast. — [Taylor & Francis ergonomics chapter](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/e/9781315098128/chapters/10.1201/b22361-52)
  - FHWA in-vehicle guidance: ≥30 arcmin for titles and key elements, ≥20 arcmin for critical dynamic elements. — [FHWA](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/98057/ch03.cfm)
- **The CSS reference pixel.** One CSS px is defined as the visual angle of a pixel at 96 dpi viewed at arm's length (≈0.0213°). — [W3C CSS Values 3, "reference pixel"](https://www.w3.org/TR/css-values-3/#reference-pixel) (not fetched)
- **Child repair strategies with voice interfaces [Moderate, preschool].** Fourteen preschoolers used a deliberately broken voice mini-game in a Sesame/PBS Kids app at home for two weeks, giving 107 failed attempts. Strategies were repetition (79% of children), increased volume (63%), and variation. The authors call for interfaces that respond, for example by re-prompting. — [Cheng et al. 2018, IDC '18, "Why Doesn't It Work?"](https://faculty.washington.edu/alexisr/quack.pdf)
- **ASR on child speech [Moderate].**
  - 2017: adult speech word error rate was under 5%, but most engines could not correctly transcribe a single child utterance. Google recognised 11.8% of constrained and about 6% of spontaneous child speech. The authors advised against relying solely on ASR. — [Kennedy et al. 2017, HRI](https://james-kennedy.github.io/publications/KennedyEtAl2017HRIFull.pdf)
  - 2024 re-test of the same data: Whisper led, recognising 60.3% of sentences correctly (barring small grammatical differences). — [Janssens et al. 2024, arXiv 2404.17394](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17394)
- **Children's turn-taking gaps [Weak–Moderate; preschool data].** Children answer simple questions faster than complex ones; delays reflect comprehension plus response planning. — [Casillas, Bobb & Clark 2016, *J. Child Language*](https://www.mpi.nl/publications/item2227956/turn-taking-timing-and-planning-early-language-acquisition)
  - Cited reference values: a median gap of 587 ms at ages 2;8–3;5, and mean gaps of 1,100–1,800 ms in preschool child–child conversation. — secondary, via [Casillas et al. / core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/210782691.pdf)
  - Adult conversation gaps average about 200 ms across languages. — [Stivers et al. 2009, *PNAS* 106(26)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903616106) (not fetched)
- **System response time [Moderate, adult; Japanese participants].** Preference for a communication robot's response time peaked at 1 s and fell off by 2 s; the robot should respond within about 2 s. A short conversational filler (Japanese "etto", "well…") moderated the negative impression of longer delays. — [Shiwa et al. 2008, HRI](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/rec/html/conf/hri/ShiwaKIIH08); [2009, *International Journal of Social Robotics*](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2FS12369-009-0012-8)
- **Classic response-time limits [Weak/Adult].** 0.1 s feels instantaneous, 1 s preserves flow of thought, and 10 s is the limit for keeping attention. — [Nielsen, "Response Times: The 3 Important Limits"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/) (not fetched)

### Inferences
- **Who the text is for.** For a voice-first 6–8 product, on-screen text is mainly for the facilitator. Any word meant for the child (a name, "Hi!", a sticker caption) should be big, short and paired with speech.
- **Computed laptop size.** By the W3C reference pixel, a cap height of 22 arcmin at arm's length is about 17 CSS px, which is roughly a 24 px font. Give child-facing words about 1.5× the adult legibility floor, at least 32 px. This reflects the beginning-reader benefit of larger text (Hughes & Wilkins 2000).
- **Computed size for a mirrored screen.** For mirroring, size text relative to the viewport. At `font-size: 5vh` (cap height about 3.5% of screen height), a 55″ TV viewed from 3 m gives about 27 arcmin, and a 14″ laptop at 60 cm gives about 38 arcmin; both clear the 20–22 arcmin threshold. Use 6vh for rooms deeper than about 4 m.
- **States must be unmistakable and immediate.** There are three conversational states plus idle. Each needs its own pose, motion and indicator, appearing within ≤100–200 ms of the trigger. The 0.1 s "instant" limit and children's demand for instant gratification support this.
- **End-of-turn detection must be more patient than adult defaults.** Children's gaps are long (preschool child–child mean 1.1–1.8 s) and they repeat or get louder when not understood (Cheng 2018). Use about 1.2–1.5 s of trailing silence for 6–8-year-olds, and give the facilitator a manual "child finished" key. Never show an error message. Repair in-character with a friendly re-prompt.
- **Thinking feedback.** Switch to "thinking" visuals immediately at end-of-turn. If response audio has not started by about 1 s, play an in-character non-verbal or verbal filler ("Hmmm…"), following Shiwa 2008. At about 3–4 s add a second, longer thinking beat. Before about 8–10 s, use a canned graceful line.

### Gaps
- NN/g's physical-development article (touch target sizes) returned 404 in this session. No numeric target sizes were verified, and they matter little here because the facilitator operates the laptop.
- No study gives 6–8-year-olds' tolerance (in seconds) for a voice agent's response delay. The 1 s / 2 s figures are from adults, and the filler effect is from Japanese adults.
- No child-specific evidence was found on the best visual design of listening/thinking/speaking indicators (e.g., light ring vs character pose). Recommendations are inferred from adult gaze and turn-taking work and children's contingency sensitivity.

## 5. Animation that conveys life (blinking, idle breathing, anticipation, squash and stretch) and evidence on animated pedagogical agents

### Takeaway
An avatar blink rate of about 18 per minute produced the friendliest impression (adults). Human rest rates are about 17/min, rising to about 26/min in conversation. Animated agents have a small but significant learning benefit that is larger for K-12 learners than older students. Agents with human-like gesture, facial expression and gaze (embodiment) outperform static ones when paired with a human-quality voice. Expressive vocal delivery measurably increased young children's engagement and retention.

### Cited Findings
- **Avatar blink rate [Moderate, adult].** Avatars (realistic human, cartoon human, animal, unidentified creature) blinked at 9, 12, 18, 24 or 36 per minute. Eighteen blinks per minute with a human-style avatar gave the friendliest impression, and blink effects were strongest for human-style avatars. — [Takashima et al. 2008, Graphics Interface, pp. 169–176](https://graphicsinterface.org/?p=4916); [dblp](https://dblp.org/rec/conf/graphicsinterface/TakashimaOYIKK08.html)
- **Human blink rates [Strong, adult].** Across 150 healthy adults, mean blink rate was 17/min at rest, about 26/min during conversation, and about 4.5/min while reading. The pattern conversation > rest > reading held in 67.3% of subjects. — [Bentivoglio et al. 1997, *Movement Disorders*, PMID 9399231](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9399231/)
- **Persona effect.** A lifelike animated agent ("Herman the Bug") in a learning environment had a strong positive effect on middle-school students' perceptions of their learning experience. — [Lester et al. 1997, CHI, DOI 10.1145/258549.258797](https://doi.org/10.1145/258549.258797) (not fetched)
- **Meta-analysis of pedagogical agents [Strong].** Across 43 studies and 3,088 participants, agents had a small but significant positive effect on learning. K-12 students benefited more than post-secondary students. Agents communicating by on-screen text outperformed narrating agents, which does not apply to pre-readers. — [Schroeder, Adesope & Gilbert 2013, *J. Educational Computing Research* 49(1):1–39](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/How-Effective-are-Pedagogical-Agents-for/99900601056901842)
  - A later three-level meta-analysis reportedly found g = 0.42 (k = 70) for K-12 learning, per a secondary citation. — [secondary source](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10591742)
- **Embodiment effect [Moderate, college students].** Over three experiments, learners did better on transfer tests when a human-voiced agent showed human-like gesture, facial expression, eye gaze and body movement. The effect appeared with a human voice but not a machine voice. The high-embodiment group also rated the agent higher socially. — [Mayer & DaPra 2012, *JEP: Applied* 18(3):239–252](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028616)
  - A later gesture meta-analysis (Davis 2018) found higher persona and learning outcomes with embodied agents. A children's study holding gaze and lip movement constant found the gesturing avatar produced more learning. — secondary, via [Educational Technology Journal 2021](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-021-00256-z)
- **Expressive voice helps young children [Moderate].** Forty-five preschoolers (mean age 5) heard a story from a robot with an expressive or a flat voice, with movements held constant. The expressive condition produced more concentration and engagement (from facial expressions), more emulation of the robot's story in retells, and better retention 4–6 weeks later (n = 34 returned). Children who answered the robot's questions identified target words better in the expressive condition. Self-reported liking did not differ. — [Kory Westlund et al. 2017, *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*, DOI 10.3389/fnhum.2017.00295](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00295)
- **The 12 principles of animation.** Squash and stretch, anticipation, staging, straight-ahead/pose-to-pose, follow-through and overlapping action, slow in/slow out, arcs, secondary action, timing, exaggeration, solid drawing and appeal. These are the craft basis for the "illusion of life". — [Thomas & Johnston 1981, *The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Illusion_of_Life); [Lasseter 1987, SIGGRAPH, DOI 10.1145/37402.37407](https://doi.org/10.1145/37402.37407) (not fetched)

### Inferences
- **Blinking.** A mean interval of about 3.3 s (about 18/min) with randomised spacing of 2–6 s. Use a slightly higher rate when the character is speaking (humans blink more in conversation). Add about 10–15% double blinks and blink on gaze shifts (shift-and-blink reads as natural). The randomisation, double-blink rate and blink-on-saccade are animation practice, not tested values.
- **Idle "breathing".** A slow scale or translate cycle of about 3–4 s (about 15–20 cycles/min) with slow-in/slow-out easing. Amplitude 1–2% of body height, plus small secondary motion (ear or antenna follow-through, about 100 ms lag). Add occasional idle micro-actions (glance, head tilt, smile) every 4–8 s, so the character never freezes.
- **Anticipation and squash/stretch.** Before speaking, a 150–250 ms anticipation: a small "inhale" squash, eyebrows lifting, mouth parting. Celebrations use squash and stretch (squash before jump, stretch on rise, squash on land). Expression changes ease over about 150–300 ms and are held at least 1 s so children can read them.
- **Embodiment.** Pair speech with brow raises on stressed words, head nods and tilts, and simple gestures if the character has hands. The embodiment effect favours this (Mayer & DaPra 2012), and it appears to depend on a human-quality voice.

### Gaps
- No child-specific study of preferred agent blink rate was found. Takashima's participants were adults.
- No verified source for blink *duration*. Typical animation practice is about 100–200 ms (close, brief hold, slightly slower open); not verified in this session.
- No evidence was found on idle-motion parameters (breathing period or amplitude) for virtual characters in children.
- Baylor & Kim (2005, *IJAIED*), on agent roles (expert/motivator/mentor) and agent appearance, was not verified in this session, so no specific claims are made from it.

## 6. Lip-sync for cartoon faces in the browser (amplitude vs viseme) and what quality children notice

### Takeaway
A cartoon mouth needs only six basic shapes (Rhubarb/Preston-Blair-style). Viseme timestamps from cloud TTS can drive these directly, with amplitude-driven opening as a fallback. Children's audiovisual temporal binding windows are wider than adults' for simple stimuli, though less so for speech. Adult broadcast thresholds (audio leading by more than about 45 ms is detectable) are a safe engineering target. Because adult facilitators will watch, aim for adult-grade sync.

### Cited Findings
- **Rhubarb Lip Sync mouth set.** It uses 6–9 mouth positions: shapes A–F are the "basic mouth shapes and the absolute minimum you have to draw", with G, H and X (idle/rest) optional extensions. It is based on the classic Hanna-Barbera/Preston Blair cartoon mouth set. — [Rhubarb Lip Sync README, D. S. Wolf](https://github.com/DanielSWolf/rhubarb-lip-sync); mirrored at [github.com/wzpan/rhubarb-lip-sync](https://github.com/wzpan/rhubarb-lip-sync)
  - Practitioner guidance: nine Preston Blair shapes cover most dialogue; simpler styles get by with five or six. — [Frameo mouth-chart guide (vendor, weak)](https://frameo.ai/en/learn/animation-mouth-chart)
- **Other viseme sets.**
  - Oculus/Meta Lipsync uses 15 visemes. — [Meta OVRLipSync viseme reference](https://developer.oculus.com/documentation/unity/audio-ovrlipsync-viseme-reference)
  - Azure Speech TTS emits timed viseme IDs (0–21, i.e., 22 visemes). — [Microsoft Learn: viseme events](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/speech-service/how-to-speech-synthesis-viseme) (not fetched)
  - Amazon Polly emits viseme "speech marks". — [AWS Polly speech marks](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/polly/latest/dg/speechmarks.html) (not fetched)
- **Children's audiovisual binding [Weak–Moderate].** Multisensory temporal binding windows were larger in children and adolescents than in adults, so younger listeners are more likely to bind asynchronous audio and visual events. The window for audiovisual *speech* matured earlier and was comparable across children, adolescents and adults. — [Hillock dissertation, Vanderbilt](https://ir.vanderbilt.edu/handle/1803/13839); [Hillock, Powers & Wallace 2011 context](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3140703)
  - Window widths depend on fitting criteria, so compare across studies with caution. — [Stanley et al. 2019](https://mail.maurer.ca/dpubs/Stanley_2019.pdf)
- **Broadcast A/V sync thresholds [Weak/Adult].** Detectability limits are sound leading the picture by about 45 ms and sound lagging by about 125 ms. Acceptability limits are about +90 ms and −185 ms. — [ITU-R BT.1359](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BT.1359/en) (not fetched; values from known standard)

### Inferences
- **Recommended approach.** Request viseme timestamps from the TTS provider. Map them to a 6-shape cartoon set (closed M/B/P; slightly open consonants; open "A/I"; wide "E"; round "O"; puckered "U/W"; plus F/V teeth-on-lip if drawn) and a rest shape X. Crossfade or tween shapes over about 40–60 ms.
- **Fallback.** Amplitude- or RMS-driven jaw opening (3–4 discrete open levels, about 30 ms attack and 80 ms release smoothing). Close the mouth on silence longer than about 120 ms. This is cheap and, for a cartoon face, adequate for children; mismatched mouth shapes are the main giveaway to adults.
- **Sync target.** Mouth shapes should never trail the audio by more than about 45 ms, since audio leading is the more noticeable error. Schedule visemes against the Web Audio clock (`AudioContext.currentTime`), not `setTimeout`. Aim for the mouth to lead the audio by 0–40 ms.
- Adults (facilitators, parents) are more sensitive than children to sync and to the uncanny valley (Brink 2019), so "adult-grade" sync is the safer target.

### Gaps
- No study was found measuring what lip-sync quality 6–8-year-olds notice or prefer for cartoon characters (viseme vs amplitude). This is a genuine evidence gap; recommendations rest on adult thresholds and developmental binding-window data.

## 7. Audio: voice qualities, speech rate, sound effects and music, loudness

### Takeaway
An expressive, warm, wide-pitch-range voice beats a flat one for young children's engagement and retention. Modern neural TTS is about as credible and effective as a human voice (adult data). Faster-than-normal speech harms 6-year-olds' comprehension, and slower speech often helps. Children need a 3–6 dB better speech-to-noise ratio than adults, so music and SFX must never compete with speech. Congruent sound and music in children's digital stories help rather than distract, but SFX can disrupt speech perception for weaker verbal processors.

### Cited Findings
- **Expressive vs flat voice [Moderate].** The expressive voice produced more engagement, better story emulation and better retention than the flat voice in preschoolers. — [Kory Westlund et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00295)
- **Modern TTS vs human voice [Moderate, adult].** A modern TTS engine matched a recorded human voice on credibility and learning, and both beat an older TTS engine. The original "voice effect" may have reflected 2000s-era synthesis. — [Craig & Schroeder 2017, *Computers & Education* 114:193–205](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10639-022-11255-6.pdf) (cited via secondary)
  - A 2019 follow-up without a visible agent found a human voice raised persona ratings but not learning. — same secondary source.
- **Speech rate in 6-year-olds [Moderate].** Six-year-olds made significantly more sentence-comprehension errors at fast speech (duration compressed to 60%) than at normal or slowed speech (expanded to 160%). — [Haake et al., summarised in Guiraud et al. 2018, "Don't speak too fast!", PMC5786310](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5786310/)
  - A Swedish study of 102 typically developing children found fast rate lowered TROG-2 grammar scores and slow rate was generally more beneficial. — same source.
- **Preferred synthetic-speech rate rises with age [Weak].** For synthetic speech (DECtalk, VeriVox), preferred presentation rate in words per minute increased with listener age across 6–85 years. — [*Augmentative and Alternative Communication* 2009](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07434610902728531)
- **Voice gender [Moderate].** Synthesized child voices on a NAO robot successfully conveyed robot age and gender, and some voices were preferred. — [Sandygulova & O'Hare 2015, ICSR, LNCS 9388](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-25554-5_59)
  - In a related study (107 children aged 5–12), 5–8-year-olds could not reliably attribute gender from a synthesized voice. Younger children preferred a robot of their own gender; 9–12-year-olds showed no preference. — [Sandygulova & O'Hare, *IJSR*](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2FS12369-018-0472-9)
- **Speech in noise [Strong].** Children need a 3–6 dB more favourable SNR than adults for comparable speech detection, word identification or sentence recognition, and this persists until about age 11–14. — [Leibold 2019, *Acoustics Today*, "Too Young for the Cocktail Party"](https://acousticstoday.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/Too-Young-for-the-Cocktail-Party-Lori-J.-Leibold.pdf)
  - In a digits-in-noise study of 112 children aged 4–12, 4-year-olds' speech reception thresholds were 3–7 dB worse than adults'. — [*American Journal of Audiology* 2016](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/pdf/10.1044/2016_AJA-15-0064)
- **Multimedia vs interactive features in children's stories [Strong].** Across 43 studies and 2,147 children, multimedia features (animated pictures, music, sound effects) were beneficial, and congruent animation, sound and music did not distract from the story. Interactive features (hotspots, games) were detrimental. SFX may disrupt speech perception for children with verbal-processing difficulties. — [Takacs, Swart & Bus 2015, *Review of Educational Research* 85(4):698–739](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.3102/0034654314566989)

### Inferences
- **Voice.** Use a warm, smiling, expressive neural TTS voice with wide pitch variation and clear articulation. Avoid a flat, assistant-style read.
  - Rate: about 10–15% slower than the engine default (roughly 0.85–0.9×). Insert 400–700 ms pauses between sentences and keep utterances short (≤12 words, one idea each).
  - These numbers are extrapolations. Children of 6 are harmed by fast speech, but no study gives an optimal words-per-minute for this age.
- **Gender.** Because 5–8-year-olds may prefer a same-gender agent and cannot reliably gender synthetic voices, a gender-ambiguous "creature" voice (moderately high pitch, non-human character) avoids alienating half the audience. This is an inference.
- **Mixing.**
  - No background music while either party speaks: the child's speech must reach the mic cleanly, and the agent's speech must reach a child who needs 3–6 dB more SNR.
  - Put short musical stingers and SFX only in gaps.
  - Duck any ambient bed at least 15–20 dB under speech, or mute it.
  - Normalise speech around −16 LUFS integrated and keep SFX peaks no louder than speech.
  - Playback at roughly normal conversational level for a child seated about 60 cm away.
  - These mixing values come from broadcast and practitioner norms, not child studies.
- Sound effects should be *congruent* (a soft "pop" when the character appears, a gentle chime when it starts listening). Avoid harsh or sudden loud sounds.

### Gaps
- No child-specific evidence was found on the best TTS pitch or the optimal words-per-minute for ages 6–8.
- No child-specific evidence was found on startle or annoyance thresholds for celebration SFX loudness.
- The ANSI S12.60 classroom SNR value (+15 dB) was not verified in this session. An educational source cites +16 dB for children vs +6 dB for adults, but that is not a primary source. — [University of Edinburgh training page](https://www.ssc.education.ed.ac.uk/courses/deaf/dnov10iii.html)

## 8. Reward and celebration design: stickers, badges, confetti, certificates, and the overjustification effect

### Takeaway
Expected, tangible, task-contingent rewards reduce young children's later free-choice engagement. Unexpected rewards and sincere, process-focused verbal acknowledgement do not, and can enhance motivation. Celebrate with brief, sincere, specific character reactions. If you give a sticker or certificate, present it as an unannounced end-of-session thank-you or memento, never as a contingent prize.

### Cited Findings
- **Overjustification effect [Strong].** Preschoolers (about 3–5) already interested in drawing were assigned to expected-award (n = 18, shown a "Good Player" certificate in advance), unexpected-award (n = 18) or no-award (n = 15) groups. One to two weeks later, the expected-award group spent significantly less free-choice time drawing. — [Lepper, Greene & Nisbett 1973, *JPSP* 28(1):129–137, DOI 10.1037/h0035519](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0035519)
  - Reported free-choice drawing time: expected about 8.59%, no-award about 16.73%, unexpected about 18.09%. These figures come from a secondary blog and were not verified against the table. — [secondary](https://teachingcs.substack.com/p/drawing-for-rewards)
- **Meta-analysis of extrinsic rewards [Strong].** Across 128 studies, free-choice intrinsic motivation fell with engagement-contingent (d = −0.40), completion-contingent (d = −0.36) and performance-contingent (d = −0.28) rewards, as well as with all tangible and all expected rewards. Tangible rewards were more detrimental for children than for college students, and verbal rewards were less enhancing for children. — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, *Psychological Bulletin* 125(6):627–668](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/)
- **Praise [Strong, review].** Praise can undermine, enhance or have no effect. When perceived as *sincere*, it benefits motivation if it attributes performance to controllable causes (effort, strategy), promotes autonomy, and builds competence without social comparison. — [Henderlong & Lepper 2002, *Psychological Bulletin* 128(5):774–795](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12206194/)
- **Generic vs process praise [Moderate, 4-year-olds].** With puppets, 24 four-year-olds praised generically ("you are a good drawer") later gave up more after criticism and did not try to fix mistakes. Those given process praise ("you did a good job drawing") persisted. — [Cimpian, Arce, Markman & Dweck 2007, *Psychological Science* 18:314–316, via BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-praise-children)
  - Even a small proportion of generic, person-focused praise reduced persistence. — [Zentall & Morris 2010, PubMed 20570281](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20570281/)
- **Interactive extras distract [Strong].** Interactive add-ons (games, hotspots) in children's digital stories were detrimental, while congruent animation and sound helped. — [Takacs, Swart & Bus 2015](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.3102/0034654314566989)
- **Children's motivation [Moderate].** NN/g notes children are entertainment-driven, with entertainment as their main goal. — [Sherwin & Nielsen 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/childrens-websites-usability-issues/)

### Inferences
- **No up-front reward announcement.** Never say "if you talk to me for 5 minutes you get a sticker", and run no points, streaks, stars-per-answer or leaderboards. That is engagement- or completion-contingent and expected, the most undermining category (d ≈ −0.40).
- **Moment-to-moment celebration** should be the character's *social* reaction:
  - a joyful expression and bounce (about 600–1,000 ms, squash and stretch);
  - a brief congruent chime (≤1 s);
  - sincere, specific, process-focused words: "You thought really hard about that!", "I love how you explained the part about your dog", "Thank you for telling me!"
  - Avoid trait praise ("You're so smart!") and over-praising trivial acts, which reads as insincere (Henderlong & Lepper 2002; Cimpian 2007).
- **Big celebration.** Reserve one bigger moment (confetti burst about 1.5–2.5 s, a short musical flourish) for the session end. Frame it as the *character's* delight and gratitude ("That was so fun talking with you!") rather than a score.
  - If a sticker or certificate is given, make it unannounced and a memento of the conversation (e.g., "Friend of [Character]" with the character's face and a sentence about something the child shared). It must not depend on performance. Unexpected rewards did not undermine motivation in Lepper et al. 1973.
- Keep celebrations non-interactive and short, so they don't become the "game" that displaces conversation (Takacs et al. 2015).

### Gaps
- The Deci et al. 1999 children-specific effect sizes for verbal rewards and for unexpected tangible rewards were not confirmed in this session. Commonly cited values (verbal praise enhancing for college students but not significantly for children; unexpected tangible rewards about d = 0) should be checked against the paper.
- No study was found specifically on confetti or celebratory animation effects in children's conversational agents.

## 9. Design implications: consolidated numbered spec

### Takeaway
The spec below translates the evidence into concrete defaults. Items marked (E) are directly evidence-backed; items marked (X) are extrapolations or engineering judgment to be validated in playtests with 6–8-year-olds.

### Inferences (numbered spec)

**Character form**
1. **Style.** Stylised cartoon (2D vector or soft-3D), non-realistic. A friendly creature or animal-like being is preferred over a realistic human. (E: Brink 2019; Woods 2006; Macdonald 1996; Borgi 2014)
2. **Proportions.**
   - Face fills about 60–70% of the character canvas, with a round or superellipse silhouette.
   - Eyes at or slightly below the head's vertical midline (high forehead); each eye about 18–25% of face width; large dark pupils with 1–2 white catch-lights.
   - Tiny or no nose; small mouth at rest; round cheeks with optional blush.
   - (E for features: Glocker 2009; Borgi 2014; Morris 1995. X for exact ratios.)
3. **Shape language.** Circles and curves everywhere. No V-brows, spikes, horns, sharp teeth or downward-pointing triangles. Brows are soft arcs; even "concerned" uses raised inner-brow arcs, not angled ones. (E, adult: Bar & Neta 2006; Larson et al. 2009/2012)

**Colour**
4. **Palette.**
   - Background: warm cream #FFF8EC (or very light sky #E8F4FD).
   - Character body: apricot #FFB86B, or coral #F7A072 / mint-teal #7FD1C4.
   - Features and ink: navy #1F2A44 (13.5:1 on cream; ≥6.9:1 on all body colours above).
   - Accent: sunny yellow #FFD166.
   - Keep black, brown and grey to small details only.
   - (E: Boyatzis & Varghese 1994; X for specific hues)
5. **State and indicator colours (Okabe–Ito, CVD-safe).**
   - Listening: blue #0072B2 (4.9:1).
   - Thinking: vermillion #D55E00 (3.7:1), or orange #E69F00 *with* a navy outline.
   - Speaking: bluish green #009E73 (3.2:1).
   - Always pair colour with distinct motion, icon and character pose. Never red vs green alone. (E: NEI about 8% of boys; Okabe & Ito; WCAG 1.4.11 ≥3:1)
6. **Confetti and celebration colours.** The Okabe–Ito set: #E69F00, #56B4E9, #009E73, #F0E442, #0072B2, #D55E00, #CC79A7.

**Text and layout**
7. **Text sizes.**
   - Child-facing text: at least 32 px on the laptop, and `font-size: max(32px, 5vh)` when mirrored (6vh for rooms deeper than about 4 m).
   - Facilitator UI text: at least 18–19 px (NN/g 14 pt ≈ 18.7 px).
   - One to three words per child-facing label, always spoken aloud too. Rounded, high-legibility sans with a single-storey "a" is optional (X).
   - (E: NN/g 14 pt; Hughes & Wilkins 2000; computed from 20–22 arcmin legibility plus the W3C reference pixel)
8. **No error messages to the child.** All failures are repaired in character ("Oops, my ears got fuzzy. Can you say that again?"). (E: NN/g; Cheng 2018)

**Animation and idle**
9. **Blink.**
   - Mean 18/min (interval about 3.3 s), randomised 2–6 s; raise to about 20–24/min while speaking.
   - About 10–15% double blinks; blink on large gaze shifts.
   - Blink duration about 120–180 ms (close about 50 ms, hold about 30 ms, open about 70 ms).
   - (E for rate: Takashima 2008; Bentivoglio 1997. X for duration and randomisation.)
10. **Breathing idle.** 3.5 s cycle (about 17/min), vertical scale 1–2% with ease-in-out. Secondary follow-through on appendages lags about 80–120 ms. (X; Thomas & Johnston principles)
11. **Idle micro-actions.** Every 4–8 s (randomised): a small glance, head tilt (≤8°), smile flicker or eyebrow lift. Pupils make tiny 1–2 px drifts every 1–3 s so the face never looks frozen. (X)
12. **Expressions.**
    - Core set: happy, calm-attentive, curious/thinking, gentle-sad/sympathetic, excited, gentle-surprise. No angry, fearful or disgusted.
    - Each expression uses at least three channels (brows + eyes + mouth) plus head motion.
    - Transitions of 150–300 ms with ease-out; hold at least 1 s.
    - (E: Lawrence 2015 / FYM 2016; Guarnera 2015; Macdonald 1996. X for timings.)
13. **Anticipation and squash/stretch.** A 150–250 ms anticipation pose before each speaking turn (slight squash plus inhale, brows up). Celebrations use squash → stretch → squash-on-land over about 600–1,000 ms. (Thomas & Johnston; X timings)
14. **Embodied speech.** While speaking, brow raises and head nods on stressed words, plus occasional gestures. This needs a human-quality voice. (E: Mayer & DaPra 2012; Davis 2018)

**Turn-taking state machine**
15. **IDLE / WAITING-FOR-CHILD.** Gaze at the child (track their face via webcam), soft smile, breathing, blinking. Optional gentle prompt after about 6–8 s of silence: a pre-recorded "Hmm, what do you think?" (X)
16. **LISTENING (child speaking).**
    - Within ≤150 ms of voice onset: lean in or scale up about 3%, eyes slightly wider, brows lifted.
    - Blue (#0072B2) "ear" or ring indicator pulses with the child's mic level, so they *see* they are heard.
    - Small nods every about 2–4 s at the child's pauses.
    - Sustained eye contact.
    - (E: contingency, Breazeal 2016; X timings)
17. **End-of-turn detection.** 1.2–1.5 s of trailing silence for ages 6–8 (vs about 0.5–0.8 s typical for adults), plus a facilitator "child finished" hotkey and a "keep listening" override. (E: child gaps 0.6–1.8 s, Casillas 2016; ASR fragility, Kennedy 2017. X for exact threshold)
18. **THINKING.**
    - Enter within ≤200 ms of end-of-turn.
    - Gaze averts up and to the side (about 15–20° equivalent), brow raised, mouth pursed or "hmm", and a slow orange/vermillion "thought" indicator (e.g., three bobbing dots in a thought bubble). Optional soft looping "thinking" sound at low level.
    - If response audio has not started by 1.0 s, play a short in-character filler ("Hmmm…", "Ooh, let me think…", 0.4–0.8 s, pre-generated so it's instant).
    - At about 3.5 s, a second filler or longer thinking beat (e.g., taps chin, eyes roll up).
    - At 8 s, a pre-recorded graceful recovery line.
    - Never exceed 10 s of silence.
    - (E: Andrist 2013; Doherty-Sneddon 2007; Shiwa 2008; Nielsen 10 s. X for 3.5 s and 8 s.)
19. **SPEAKING.** Gaze returns to the child at the first syllable. Green (#009E73) speaking indicator or mouth glow. Viseme-driven mouth. Embodied gestures (item 14). At the end, a 300–500 ms "your turn" cue: a small head tilt plus raised brows plus a soft listening chime, then LISTENING. (X; E for gaze-back, Andrist 2013)
20. **Barge-in.** If the child starts talking while the character is speaking, stop speech within about 200–300 ms and switch to LISTENING. Children repeat and get louder when ignored. (E: Cheng 2018; X timing)

**Lip-sync**
21. **Primary: TTS viseme events.** Use Azure viseme IDs or Polly viseme speech marks, mapped to 6 cartoon mouth shapes (Rhubarb A–F) plus rest X. Tween 40–60 ms between shapes. Schedule on the `AudioContext` clock. (E: Rhubarb set; X for mapping and tween)
22. **Fallback: amplitude-driven.** RMS over a 20–30 ms window maps to 3–4 jaw-open levels (attack about 30 ms, release about 80 ms). Mouth closes after more than 120 ms of silence. Lip-rounding comes from viseme hints if available. (X)
23. **Sync budget.** Mouth onset may lead audio by 0–40 ms and must never lag it by more than 45 ms. Measure end-to-end on the target laptop and on any HDMI mirror; TV processing can add audio or video delay. (E, adult: ITU-R BT.1359; children's wider binding window gives extra margin, Hillock)

**Audio**
24. **Voice.**
    - Warm, smiling, highly expressive neural TTS with wide pitch range; moderately high, gender-ambiguous "creature" timbre.
    - Rate 0.85–0.9× engine default; 400–700 ms between sentences; ≤12 words per sentence; one question per turn.
    - (E: Kory Westlund 2017; Craig & Schroeder 2017; Haake via Guiraud 2018; Sandygulova. X for numeric settings.)
25. **Mix.**
    - No music under speech.
    - Speech normalised to about −16 LUFS; SFX and stingers peak at or below speech level and last ≤1 s (micro) or ≤2.5 s (finale).
    - Any ambient bed ducked at least 15–20 dB under speech or muted during both listening and speaking (it also protects mic input for ASR).
    - Soft, rounded timbres (marimba, bells, soft synths); no harsh buzzers or "wrong" sounds.
    - (E: children need +3–6 dB SNR, Leibold 2019; SFX may disrupt speech perception, Takacs 2015. X for LUFS values.)
26. **State earcons.** Distinct, gentle, under 300 ms cues for "I'm listening" (rising two-note) and "your turn". Thinking may have a very quiet loop. Keep them consistent so children learn them. (X)

**Rewards and celebration**
27. **No announced rewards.** No points, streaks, stars-per-answer or progress meters tied to talking. (E: Lepper 1973; Deci 1999 d ≈ −0.40 for engagement-contingent rewards)
28. **Micro-celebrations.** Character joy reaction (bounce, sparkle eyes, 600–1,000 ms) plus a ≤1 s chime plus sincere, specific, process or effort praise, and gratitude for sharing. Ration it to about 1 per 2–3 exchanges to avoid insincerity. Never use trait praise ("so smart"). (E: Henderlong & Lepper 2002; Cimpian 2007; Zentall & Morris 2010. X for rate.)
29. **Finale.** One end-of-session celebration: confetti 1.5–2.5 s in Okabe–Ito colours, a 2 s musical flourish, and the character thanking the child. An *unannounced* printable sticker or certificate is framed as a memento of the chat, the same for every child, with no performance grading. (E: unexpected rewards not undermining, Lepper 1973. X for durations.)
30. **Keep celebration non-interactive and short.** No mini-games inside the conversation. (E: Takacs 2015: interactive features detrimental)

### Gaps
- Most numeric timings (blink duration, breathing period, filler thresholds, end-of-turn silence, LUFS targets, proportion ratios) are extrapolations. They should be validated in playtests with 6–8-year-olds by measuring:
  - interruptions and talk-overs;
  - repeat/louder repair events (Cheng 2018 categories);
  - smiles and engagement;
  - child-reported liking;
  - facilitator overrides.
- No direct evidence was found on what lip-sync quality, agent blink rate or response latency 6–8-year-olds prefer. These are flagged as research gaps rather than settled specs.
