# How Young Children (5–9, focus 6–8) Perceive and Interact with Conversational Agents, Voice Assistants, Social Robots and AI, and How to Teach Them What AI Is

Scope note: Findings are from peer-reviewed papers unless flagged (arXiv preprint, press release, law-firm summary, or aggregator). Sample sizes and ages are given for each study where found. Where a detail came only from an abstract or secondary summary, that is stated.

## 1. How do 6–8-year-olds perceive agents (animism, mind, feelings, intelligence, trust), and what changes between 6 and 8?

### Takeaway
Most 6–8-year-olds know a talking device is "a machine/AI" and not human, but many still say it might think, decide, or have feelings ("dual consciousness"). They often judge it smarter than themselves, extend it moral regard, and trust it for facts. Between roughly 5 and 8, children get better at separating AI from people. By 7–8 they choose the device for facts and a human for personal information, and the human–AI gap in mind attribution widens with age and language skill. Uncanny-valley discomfort with very human-like robots does not appear until about age 9.

### Cited Findings
**Classic voice-assistant and robot studies**
- **Druga, Williams, Breazeal & Resnick (2017, IDC), "Hey Google, is it OK if I eat you?"** Children aged 3–10 used Alexa, Google Home, Cozmo and the Julie chatbot. The abstract says 26 children; the MIT blog says 27 children aged 4–10, so the two sources disagree. Older children (6–10) mostly thought the agents were *more intelligent than themselves*, citing the agents' access to information. Younger children were less sure and tested the agents physically ("eat an apple", "open the door"). Most participants agreed the agents were friendly and trustworthy. — [MIT Media Lab publication page](https://www-prod.media.mit.edu/publications/hey-google-is-it-ok-if-i-eat-you-initial-explorations-in-child-agent-interaction/); [DOI 10.1145/3078072.3084330](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F3078072.3084330); [MIT blog summary](https://www.media.mit.edu/posts/kids-ai-devices/)
- **Druga et al. (2017), quotes and other observations.**
  - Children attributed feelings and minds based on expressiveness. A 6-year-old said a robot had a mind "because then they have [expressions]"; a 9-year-old said Cozmo "has feelings."
  - Children used "he" and "she" interchangeably for the devices.
  - Younger children focused on sensory and playful interactions and more often missed Cozmo's facial expressions. Older children discussed capabilities and intelligence.
  - Some children preferred Alexa's "energetic" voice over Google Home's more human-like one.
  - [MIT blog](https://www.media.mit.edu/posts/kids-ai-devices/)
- **Andries & Robertson (2023, *Computers and Education: AI* 5, 100176).** 166 Scottish children aged 6–11, using questionnaires and interviews; 93% had a smart speaker at home, mostly Alexa.
  - Children tended to rate the devices as smarter than themselves and were unsure about the devices' inner life.
  - About two in three thought the devices might have feelings or make their own decisions, with many "maybe" answers.
  - Only about 1% classified them as "human."
  - About 8 in 10 knew the devices were examples of AI.
  - [arXiv 2305.05597](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05597); [Edinburgh Research Explorer](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/alexa-doesnt-have-that-many-feelings-childrens-understanding-of-a-2/); [University of Edinburgh news, 2024](https://www.ed.ac.uk/news/2024/children-may-overestimate-smart-speakers-abilities)
- **Flanagan, Wong & Kushnir (2023, *Developmental Psychology* 59(6):1017–1031).** 127 children aged 4–11 at a science museum watched 20-second clips of Alexa, Roomba and Nao.
  - Children credited Alexa, which *talks*, with more human-like thoughts and emotions (for example, feeling upset after mistreatment) than Roomba.
  - Most believed neither device could feel physical sensations such as pain or tickling.
  - Children of all ages said it was wrong to hit or yell at the machines.
  - [PubMed 37036664](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37036664/); [Duke news](https://today.duke.edu/2023/04/kids-judge-alexa-smarter-roomba-say-both-deserve-kindness)
- **Girouard-Hallam, Streble & Danovitch (2021, *Human Behavior and Emerging Technologies* 3(5):1118–1131).** Studied children's mental, social and moral attributions to a familiar voice assistant. Per a secondary review, kindergarten-aged children attribute more experiences, mental abilities and moral deservedness to smart speakers than older children do. By about age 6, children know smart speakers are not living things but still credit them with human-like qualities. The full text was not accessed. — [DOI 10.1002/hbe2.321, as summarized in a Cambridge-hosted review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/00529D3B294882679C6C773B99A4D1C0)

**Trust**
- **Girouard-Hallam & Danovitch (2022, *Developmental Psychology* 58(4):646–661).** Two studies compared a novel voice assistant with a human informant, with children aged 4–5 and 7–8 from predominantly White, upper-middle-class families.
  - *7–8-year-olds* trusted the voice assistant more for factual information and the human more for personal information about the experimenter.
  - In Study 2, older children increasingly chose to ask the assistant for facts and the human for personal information.
  - Choices did not track children's attributions of thinking ability, or whether they had a voice assistant at home.
  - [DOI 10.1037/dev0001318 (record)](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1508663845096112128); [library record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_2644364364)

**Robovie and "new ontological category"**
- **Kahn et al. (2012, *Developmental Psychology* 48(2):303–314), "Robovie, you'll have to go into the closet now."** 90 children aged 9, 12 and 15. Each had a 15-minute interaction, which ended when an experimenter interrupted Robovie's game and put it in a closet, followed by a 50-minute interview.
  - About 80% said Robovie was intelligent and 60% said it had feelings.
  - Among *9-year-olds*: 93% said intelligent and 67% said it had feelings. Among 15-year-olds: 70% and 43%.
  - 88% said Robovie was treated unfairly when it lost its turn, and 54% said it was not right to put it in the closet.
  - [Paper PDF](https://empdb.apps.umt.edu/storage/facultyfiles/4421/Robovie_Closet_Study_Developmental_Psych_2012.pdf); [UW News](https://www.washington.edu/news/2012/04/05/children-perceive-humanoid-robot-as-emotional-moral-being); [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/automaton/robotics/artificial-intelligence/do-kids-care-if-their-robot-friend-gets-stuffed-into-a-closet)
- **Severson & Carlson (2010, *Neural Networks* 23(8–9):1099–1103).** A theory paper asking whether children's attributions to personified robots reflect genuine belief or pretense ("behaving *as* or behaving *as if*"). It proposes that personified robots may form a *new ontological category* between animate and inanimate. A 2018 follow-up poster with 90 children aged 5 and 7 found mixed results: children treat robots as something other than a simple category fit. — [Paper PDF](https://empdb.apps.umt.edu/storage/facultyfiles/4421/severson-carlson-2010.pdf); [UM poster record](https://scholarworks.umt.edu/umcur/2018/pmposters/14)

**Uncanny valley develops with age**
- **Brink, Gray & Wellman (2019, *Child Development* 90(4):1202–1214), "Creepiness creeps in."** 240 children aged 3–18 saw a machine-like or a very human-like robot.
  - The uncanny valley appears at **about age 9**. Before 9, robot type did not predict creepiness (age 4: b = −0.08, p = .57; age 8: b = .13, p = .11). At 9 it did (b = .18, p = .02), and the effect kept growing to age 16 (b = .55).
  - For ages 4–9, *more* attribution of mind predicted *less* uncanniness (age 4: b = −0.55, p < .001; age 9: b = −0.23, p = .04). In older children this relationship trended toward positive.
  - [DOI 10.1111/cdev.12999](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/75a84df8-72e0-4147-9dea-6d71c42035df); [author manuscript PDF](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/handle/2027.42/150519/cdev12999_am.pdf?sequence=1)

**LLM-era studies**
- **Xu, Thomas, Yu & Pan (2025, *Computers in Human Behavior: Artificial Humans* 4, 100135).** 119 children aged 4–8 co-created stories with one of three partners: a **GPT-4 agent via a speaker**, a physically present human, or a hidden human heard only through a speaker.
  - Compared with the AI, a present human scored +0.75 for agency and +1.94 for experience on a 3-point scale (both p < .001).
  - A hidden human was rated higher than the AI on experience (b = 0.37, p = .03) but *not* on agency (b = 0.13, p = .49; Bayes factor = 0.30 supporting no difference).
  - The present-human-versus-AI gap *widened with age* (agency b = .26, p = .04; experience b = .31, p = .008). Children with stronger language skills differentiated more.
  - Without a visible body, children judged human-likeness from linguistic and paralinguistic cues. The AI's faster articulation rate and less readable language fully mediated its lower "experience" ratings compared with the hidden human.
  - Mind perception did *not* correlate with communicative behavior. Response rates were above 90% in all conditions; substantive responses were 94% with a present human and about 80% with the AI or hidden human.
  - [Author PDF (NSF PAR)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10613107)
- **Bickham et al. (2024), as cited in Xu et al. 2025.** A US survey found that nearly half of children aged 3–9 interacted with AI agents daily, mostly voice assistants, with generative chatbots increasing. — [Xu et al. 2025 PDF](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10613107)
- **Kim, Chin, Xie, Brady, Yeh & Yang (2025, arXiv 2512.02179; not peer-reviewed).** 23 children aged 5–6 did storytelling in three conditions: an LLM chatbot alone, a parent alone, and the AI plus the parent.
  - Parents were rated more anthropomorphic overall, but the AI received relatively high ratings for *perceptive* abilities ("it can hear/see") and *epistemic* states ("it knows").
  - Anthropomorphism was not associated with conversational turn count.
  - In an exploratory result, higher activity in the right dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (a brain region tied to thinking about others' minds) during AI-only sessions correlated with higher end-of-session "scared" ratings.
  - Parent co-presence "may help some children interpret and regulate novel AI interactions."
  - [arXiv 2512.02179](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2512.02179v2)
- **Jayathilake & Ma (2026, arXiv 2607.18250; preprint systematic review).** Reviews 35 empirical studies (2022 to August 2025) of children and LLM chatbots.
  - The main drivers of anthropomorphism were human-like persona construction (19 studies), "friend"-style companionship (16), adaptive scaffolding (14), and non-human embodiment (10).
  - "Dual consciousness" appeared in 8 studies: children know the bot is programmed yet still attribute feelings to it.
  - Children explain bot failures with human narratives such as "it's tired" or "it's hungry."
  - Children in the preoperational stage (about 2–7) did *not* show recognition of disclaimers such as "As an AI language model…"; children in the concrete-operational stage (about 7–11) did.
  - The authors conclude that "the conversational layer is the anthropomorphic layer," so de-humanized visuals alone will not stop anthropomorphism.
  - [arXiv 2607.18250](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.18250v1)
- **Kosoy, Jeong, Sinha, Gopnik & Kraljic (2024, arXiv 2405.13081; preprint).** More than 40 children aged 5–12. Children associated AI with more positive than negative adjectives and found AI "less scary" after interacting with ChatGPT and DALL-E. — [arXiv 2405.13081](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.13081v1)

**Learning about AI changes perceptions**
- **Williams, Park & Breazeal (2019, CHI), "A is for Artificial Intelligence" (PopBots).** Preschool and kindergarten children.
  - The MIT DSpace abstract says children who scored *worse* on AI assessments saw robots as toys not as smart as themselves, while those who scored *better* saw robots as people *smarter* than themselves.
  - The MIT Media Lab summary instead frames the split by age, and the sources disagree on direction.
  - [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/137141); [MIT Media Lab summary (conflicting framing)](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/a-is-for-ai/)

**Aggregator summary**
- **Children and Screens (2026), "Chatbots, AI Companions, and Youth: Research at a Glance".**
  - "Children understand that voice assistants are AI, yet often overattribute intelligence and human-like qualities to them."
  - Children perceive AI media characters as more human-like when the interaction is real-time and responsive.
  - [Children and Screens PDF](https://www.childrenandscreens.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Chatbots-AI-Companions-and-Youth-Research-at-a-Glance.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a 6–8-year-old, the "wow, it's talking to me" moment is very likely. Talking alone raises mind attribution (Flanagan 2023), and adding vision (webcam) and personal knowledge will push perceived perceptive and epistemic abilities higher still (Kim 2025). The design risk is over-attribution, not under-attribution.
- Age 6 versus 8 matters:
  - **6-year-olds** are still near the preoperational/concrete transition. They are less likely to process verbal disclaimers (Jayathilake & Ma 2026) and more likely to attribute mind and moral standing (Girouard-Hallam 2021).
  - **7–8-year-olds** start to separate domains: they trust AI for facts and humans for personal matters (Girouard-Hallam & Danovitch 2022), and they differentiate AI from humans more sharply (Xu 2025).
  - So the explanation for a 6-year-old must be more concrete, show rather than tell, and be repeated by the facilitator.
- A cartoon, non-human avatar will not trigger uncanny-valley discomfort in this age group (Brink 2019). For under-9s, attributing *more* mind actually makes a robot feel *less* creepy, so a friendly expressive character will be well received. That is exactly why its honesty about not having feelings matters.
- Children will say it is "wrong" to be mean to the agent (Flanagan 2023; Kahn 2012). The agent should not exploit this with guilt or sadness cues.

### Gaps
- Xu & Warschauer (2020, CHI), "What are you talking to?": children aged 3–6 with Google Home (DOI 10.1145/3313831.3376416). Full results and sample size were not retrieved; search snippets confirm only that the study examined animate and artifact attributions.
- Lovato & Piper (2015, IDC), "Siri, is this you?": the title and findings could not be verified in this session.
- No study was found that specifically tests 6–8-year-olds with an *animated, webcam-seeing LLM avatar* that knows personal facts about them. The closest are Xu 2025 (GPT-4 voice only), Kim 2025 (LLM storytelling, ages 5–6) and Elinor (TV character; see Section 3).
- Full results of Severson & Carlson 2010 were not retrieved.

## 2. Speech recognition breakdowns and repair: how children repair, how accurate child ASR is, and how agents should respond

### Takeaway
Automatic speech recognition (ASR) for young children has improved a lot (Whisper-class models), but it is still far from adult accuracy. For children around 5, even the best 2024 model fully transcribed only about 37% of utterances, or about 60% when small grammatical slips are allowed. Children are persistent, cheerful repairers. They mostly repeat themselves, get louder, or rephrase; they rarely ask adults for help; and they wait a long time for a reply. Frustration still appears in about a quarter of failed exchanges, and a character that obviously isn't listening quickly loses them. Agents should design for *collaborative repair*: reformulate what they did understand, offer choices, blame themselves rather than the child, and treat the adult and child as a joint "user."

### Cited Findings
**How children repair**
- **Cheng, Yen, Chen, Chen & Hiniker (2018, IDC), "Why doesn't it work?"** A two-week home deployment with 14 preschoolers. A tablet game contained a deliberately broken voice mini-game ("say quack"), yielding 107 audio samples of failed attempts.
  - Repair strategies: **repetition 79%** (every child used it), **increased volume 63%** (children shouted, adults did not), **variation in word choice or pronunciation 34%**. Children often combined strategies.
  - Children asked an adult for help in only 6 of 107 samples, even though parents were present in 54%.
  - No audible frustration in 78% of samples; frustration was detectable in 23%. Children gave up in 18 instances.
  - Children waited several seconds, sometimes about 10, before repeating, in a turn-taking "stop-and-go" pattern. They almost always answered the app's follow-up prompts even after repeated failure.
  - Parents moved through four stages: suggesting, intervening, resignation, and declaring the interaction unrepairable. They began by assuming the *child* was at fault and later blamed the device.
  - Design ideas from the authors:
    - Design for *collaborative repair* rather than only better ASR.
    - Treat the parent–child dyad as the "user."
    - Have the device reformulate what it did understand, e.g., "play five… monkeys" when it missed "lil."
    - Citing prior work, children try to repair with people more than 99% of the time when misunderstood.
  - [Paper PDF](https://faculty.washington.edu/alexisr/quack.pdf); [UW News](https://washington.edu/news/?p=58399)
- **Xu, Vigil, Bustamante & Warschauer (2022, CHI), "Elinor's Talking to Me!"** In a *semi-interactive* version of the show, the character asked questions but did not understand answers. Children "quickly lost interest" in responding once they realized she could not comprehend them. The fully conversational version improved science learning (see Section 3). — [NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10346104); [The Conversation summary](https://theconversation.com/kids-cartoon-characters-that-use-ai-to-customize-responses-help-children-learn-204282)

**How accurate child ASR is**
- **Kennedy et al. (2017, ACM/IEEE HRI), "Child speech recognition in human-robot interaction."**
  - Adult word error rate (WER) was below 5%, but most commercial engines could not correctly transcribe a single child utterance.
  - The best engine (Google) recognized 11.8% of constrained child speech and about 6% of spontaneous child speech.
  - The authors recommended against relying on ASR in child–robot interaction at that time.
  - [Plymouth research portal](https://researchportal.plymouth.ac.uk/en/publications/child-speech-recognition-in-human-robot-interaction-evaluations-a/); [arXiv 2404.17394, summarizing 2017 results](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.17394v1)
- **Janssens, Verhelst, Abbo, Ren, Pinto Bernal & Belpaeme (2024, International Symposium on Technological Advances in HRI), "Child speech recognition in HRI: Problem solved?"** 11 children, mean age 4.9, with 50 repeated sentences and 222 spontaneous sentences.
  - Fully correct utterances: **Whisper large-v3 36.8%**, Azure 23.5%, Google 9.6% (Google scored 7.5% in 2017).
  - "Relaxed" accuracy, ignoring small grammatical differences: **Whisper 60.3%**, Azure 43.0%, Google 14.7%.
  - Normalized edit distance (lower is better): 0.14 for Whisper versus 0.38 for the best 2017 engine.
  - The robot's built-in microphones performed worst because of motor and fan noise; external microphones were significantly better.
  - Local Whisper on a GPU ran in under a second; the medium and large models on CPU took 17.5 s and 30.5 s. The authors cite 700–1000 ms as acceptable dialogue latency.
  - Recommendations: use local models and external microphones, and let dialogue management (for example, LLMs) compensate for imperfect transcripts.
  - [arXiv 2404.17394](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.17394v1)
- **Attia et al. (2024, AAAI/ACM AIES), "Kid-Whisper."** Fine-tuning on the MyST children's corpus (children in roughly grades 3–5) cut Whisper's WER from 13.93% to 9.11% (Small) and from 13.23% to 8.61% (Medium). Gains carried over to unseen datasets. MyST is a children's science-tutoring speech corpus; its exact age range was not verified here. These WERs should not be assumed to hold for spontaneous speech from 6-year-olds in a noisy room. — [AIES proceedings](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/view/31618); [arXiv 2309.07927](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07927v3)
- **Jain et al. (2024, *IEEE Access*).** Fine-tuning Whisper on native and non-native English child speech gave 29–89% relative WER improvements, using as little as 10% of the unseen non-native data. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/0511c1c47cad47ab8ad278b14388fd9e)
- **Belpaeme et al. (2018, *Science Robotics*).** The review concluded that speech recognition was "still insufficiently robust" to understand young children's spoken utterances. — [Plymouth repository](https://pearl.plymouth.ac.uk/handle/10026.1/13427); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/08/180815141433.htm)

**Children's questions to voice assistants**
- **Lovato, Piper & Wartella (2019, IDC), "Hey Google, do unicorns exist?"** A two-week home deployment with 18 families with children aged 5–6, focused on what children ask. Later literature reports that children got an answer only about half the time. Assistants often reply "I don't know" to questions about Santa or the tooth fairy. — [DOI 10.1145/3311927.3323150 (PDF)](https://www.csee.umbc.edu/courses/graduate/691/spring23/pdf/3311927.3323150.pdf); [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2019/12/06/131668/why-kids-dont-trust-alexa/)
- **Lovato & Piper (2019, *Frontiers in Psychology*), review of young children and voice search.** Children had difficulty choosing which words to use and breaking a complex query into multiple steps. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00008/full)

### Inferences
- Plan for at least 1 in 3 child utterances being imperfectly transcribed, even with Whisper-class ASR. Inference from Janssens 2024, whose sample was younger (mean 4.9); 6–8-year-olds should do somewhat better, but a busy room and a laptop microphone will do worse.
- An LLM that can work out intent from a noisy transcript, plus questions with constrained answers, is the most robust approach (Janssens 2024).
- Children will repeat louder and wait. The agent should leave a generous silence window and not barge in. It should take the blame ("my computer ears missed that") so the child does not conclude they are "bad at talking." Parents in Cheng 2018 initially blamed the child.
- Because children seldom ask the adult for help (6/107), the *facilitator* should be scripted to step in after about 2 failed turns. The agent can also invite this: "Can your helper tell me what you said?"
- Never let the character keep talking as if it understood when it didn't. Elinor's semi-interactive condition shows that children disengage quickly when they notice they are not being heard.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed WER benchmark was found specifically for *spontaneous speech from 6–8-year-olds* with current models (Whisper, GPT-4o-transcribe, etc.) in classroom or noisy conditions.
- No study was found comparing specific repair *phrasings* (e.g., self-blame versus a neutral "say again") on child frustration. The recommendation rests on Cheng 2018's design suggestions and on general child-language research.
- Latency tolerance for 6–8-year-olds specifically was not found. The 700–1000 ms figure is a general dialogue-system figure (Janssens 2024). Cheng 2018 suggests children tolerate long waits.

## 3. Learning and engagement with conversational agents: which agent behaviours help, and does embodiment (face) matter versus voice-only?

### Takeaway
A well-scripted conversational agent that asks questions and gives *elaborative, contingent feedback* can match a human partner on short-term comprehension. Children engage with it about as readily, though their responses are shorter and less lexically rich than with a human. What drives benefit is dialogue (questions plus feedback), not the partner's identity. Physical or visual presence and expressive, contingent nonverbal behaviour raise engagement and perceived "mind." Parent or adult co-presence appears to help young children interpret and regulate novel AI interactions.

### Cited Findings
**Conversational agents as reading and learning partners**
- **Xu, Aubele, Vigil, Bustamante, Kim & Warschauer (2022, *Child Development* 93(2):e149–e167).** 122 children aged 3–6 were recruited and 117 analyzed (mean 58 months). A 2×2 design crossed partner (smart-speaker agent vs. adult) with reading style (dialogic vs. non-dialogic).
  - The script used 9 open-ended questions, 6 low-cognitive-demand and 3 high-demand. Feedback was *elaborative*: it acknowledged the child's answer, then explained it.
  - Dialogic reading raised comprehension by β = 0.51 SD (p < .001). The partner (agent vs. human) had no significant effect (β = −0.14, p = .25), and the agent's dialogic effect was comparable to the human's.
  - The effect worked through engagement: more narrative-relevant vocalizations and fewer irrelevant ones.
  - The age × dialogic interaction was moderate in size (0.42) but not significant, suggesting older children may benefit more.
  - [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/1rw6t612); [PubMed 34748214](https://www.pubmed.gov/34748214/)
- **Xu et al. (2021, *Computers & Education*), "Same benefits, different communication patterns."** Guided conversation with the agent supported comprehension as well as an adult did. Children's answers to the agent were *more intelligible*, while answers to adults showed *higher productivity, lexical diversity and topical relevance*. — [Publication page](https://researcher.draco.res.ibm.com/publications/same-benefits-different-communication-patterns-comparing-childrens-reading-with-a-conversational-agent-vs-a-human-partner)
- **Ying Xu (2024, Joan Ganz Cooney Center essay).** Children who talked with AI comprehended stories better and learned more vocabulary than those who only listened, but AI "still falls short" at drawing out child-driven dialogue. — [Joan Ganz Cooney Center](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/2024/10/07/different-but-complementary/)
- **Xu, Vigil, Bustamante & Warschauer (2022, CHI), "Elinor's Talking to Me!"** An animated PBS KIDS character, powered by a conversational agent, listened to children's answers and gave specific feedback or extra clues. Children in the conversational version scored higher on the immediate science assessment than those who watched the broadcast version. A later AERA 2023 study had 240 children in three groups of 80. — [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10346104); [The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/kids-cartoon-characters-that-use-ai-to-customize-responses-help-children-learn/)

**Presence, embodiment and co-presence**
- **Xu et al. (2025, *CHB: Artificial Humans*).** In a GPT-4 storytelling study (N = 119, ages 4–8):
  - Physical human presence was the dominant cue for perceived mind. Without it, speech features (articulation rate, readability) drove perceptions.
  - Substantive response rates were 94% with a present human versus about 80% with the voice-only AI.
  - The authors caution that adding realistic human embodiment to AI could trigger uncanny-valley mismatch.
  - [NSF PAR PDF](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10613107)
- **Kim et al. (2025, arXiv; 5–6-year-olds, N = 23).** Adding a parent alongside the AI changed children's brain responses (a different right dorsomedial prefrontal pattern in the AI-plus-parent condition), and the authors suggest designing for parent–AI co-use. — [arXiv 2512.02179](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2512.02179v2)
- **Horowitz-Kraus, Kenett, Link-Sourani, Ashqar & Farah (2026, *Cerebral Cortex*, DOI 10.1093/cercor/bhag167).** 15 first graders (mean age about 6.4) and 16 adults each had a **5-minute spoken conversation with ChatGPT** in an fMRI scanner. Children were told they would talk to "the smartest computer in the world."
  - Children showed weaker coupling between the brain's control and attention networks than adults, despite similar creativity scores.
  - The authors recommend that adults sit with young children, question the answers, and treat AI as a shared activity.
  - Caveats: there was no control condition and the sample was small.
  - [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/brain-scans-suggest-first-graders-dont-engage-their-control-networks-like-adults-when-talking-to-chatgpt/)
- **Children and Screens (2026; aggregator).** One cited finding: 3–7-year-olds learned more literacy skills from parents, and preferred learning from them, compared with a conversational AI. Other cited studies found conversational AI can increase parent–child dialogue during reading. — [Children and Screens PDF](https://www.childrenandscreens.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Chatbots-AI-Companions-and-Youth-Research-at-a-Glance.pdf)

**Voice quality**
- **Druga et al. (2017).** Children preferred Alexa's more energetic voice over Google Home's more human-like voice. The authors argue speech quality strongly shapes how children judge an agent. — [MIT blog](https://www.media.mit.edu/posts/kids-ai-devices/)

### Inferences
- In a 5-minute demo, the highest-value behaviours are:
  - frequent, simple, *answerable* questions (mostly low-demand, a few open-ended);
  - specific feedback that uses the child's own words, which shows the agent heard them;
  - the child's name used occasionally, not every turn;
  - slower, simpler speech than adult default text-to-speech.
- Personal facts such as parents' jobs are best used as *conversation hooks* ("I heard your mom is a nurse. What do nurses do?"), not as displays of omniscience. This keeps the child talking (engagement) rather than just being impressed.
- An animated face plus contingent reactions (nodding or reacting when the child speaks) should help engagement relative to voice-only. This is inferred from the robotics evidence in Section 4 and Xu 2025's presence effect. However, the character's "face" should be clearly non-human to avoid pushing children toward treating it as a person.
- The facilitator should be positioned as a co-user, not just an operator. Co-presence is supported by Kim 2025, Horowitz-Kraus 2026 and Cheng 2018.

### Gaps
- No direct RCT was found comparing an animated on-screen avatar versus voice-only for the *same* LLM agent with 6–8-year-olds.
- Evidence on the effect of *using the child's name* specifically was not found in this research pass. Gordon et al. 2016 (Section 4) covers affective personalization but not name use.
- The full text of the Elinor CHI paper (sample size, ages, effect sizes) was not retrieved.

## 4. Social robots for kids: expressiveness, contingency, personalization and the novelty effect

### Takeaway
Physically present, expressive, *contingent* agents get more attention, trust and learning from young children. Children treat robots as informants, prefer the one whose nonverbal behaviour responds to them, and learn more vocabulary from expressive storytelling. Personalizing affective feedback improves children's emotional engagement over time. Benefits are real but narrow, and engagement and anthropomorphism tend to peak early and decline (novelty effect). For a one-off 5-minute demo, novelty works in the designer's favour but inflates children's impressions.

### Cited Findings
- **Belpaeme, Kennedy, Ramachandran, Scassellati & Tanaka (2018, *Science Robotics* 3(21), eaat5954).** A review of more than 100 published studies.
  - Social robots have raised cognitive and affective outcomes and matched human tutoring on restricted tasks such as vocabulary and prime numbers. The effect is largely attributed to their *physical presence*.
  - Learners are positive about the experience, while parents and teachers are more cautious.
  - Speech recognition for young children is a key technical bottleneck.
  - [Plymouth repository](https://pearl.plymouth.ac.uk/handle/10026.1/13427); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/08/180815141433.htm)
- **Breazeal, Harris, DeSteno, Kory Westlund, Dickens & Jeong (2016, *Topics in Cognitive Science* 8(2):481–491).** Children aged 3–5 learned about unfamiliar animals from two robots. Children treated the robots as informants: they told them things, asked them questions, and remembered what the robots said. They attended most to, and were most receptive to, the robot with *more nonverbal contingency*. — [MIT DSpace (open access)](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/108204); [MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/young-children-treat-robots-as-informants)
- **Kory Westlund et al. (2017, *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*), "Flat vs. expressive storytelling."** 45 preschoolers.
  - Children liked and learned from the expressive and flat-voice robots about equally.
  - In the expressive condition children were *more concentrated and engaged*, judged from facial expressions; they emulated the robot's story more in retells and gave longer delayed retells.
  - Among children who answered the robot's questions, those in the expressive condition were more likely to identify target vocabulary words.
  - [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00295/full)
- **Kory-Westlund & Breazeal (2019, *Frontiers in Robotics and AI*).** A long-term study with 17 preschoolers. Children who emulated more of the robot's phrases scored higher on vocabulary post-tests. — [Frontiers PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frobt.2019.00081/pdf)
- **Gordon, Spaulding, Kory Westlund, Lee, Plummer, Martinez, Das & Breazeal (2016, AAAI).** 34 preschoolers over two months worked with an autonomous robot tutor that read facial expressions (valence and engagement) and personalized its affective feedback through reinforcement learning. Children with the personalizing robot showed a significant increase in valence (positive affect) compared with a non-personalizing robot, and learned new words across sessions. — [AAAI proceedings](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/9914)
- **Novelty effect, from a 2024 longitudinal-study paper reviewing prior work.** Children's attention and engagement decrease with prolonged interaction with robots. Social responses to robot tutors fell across sessions, and children reported more boredom after long exposure in schools. — [arXiv 2404.10593](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.10593)
- **Van Straten et al.** Their work shows that anthropomorphism and relationship formation with robots are malleable (see Section 6 on transparency). — [DOI 10.1145/3365668](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F3365668)

### Inferences
- The animated character should react visibly and contingently to the child: looking toward the child's face (possible with the webcam), animating when the child speaks, and showing expressive but not exaggerated "emotional" reactions. This is the best-supported engagement lever (Breazeal 2016; Kory Westlund 2017).
- Expressive prosody helps engagement and recall but not liking (Kory Westlund 2017). Use a warm, lively voice without an ultra-realistic human voice; children may prefer "energetic" over "human-like" (Druga 2017).
- For a single 5-minute session, novelty guarantees attention. The ending explanation is therefore the main counterweight to inflated impressions, because the session will not last long enough for children's own calibration through mistakes and boredom to happen.

### Gaps
- The quantitative effect sizes in Belpaeme et al. 2018 are not confirmed here. They are often cited as roughly d ≈ 0.70 cognitive and d ≈ 0.59 affective, but this was not verified in this session.
- The primary novelty-effect papers (Kanda et al. 2004 on long-term robot playmates; Leite et al. 2013 survey) were not retrieved.
- A six-wave panel study of 8–9-year-olds reportedly found anthropomorphism peaked after the first robot interaction and then declined (likely van Straten et al., *International Journal of Social Robotics* 2024, DOI 10.1007/s12369-024-01155-9). This surfaced in search results but was not verified.

## 5. AI literacy for young children: which concepts 5–8-year-olds can grasp, and recommended analogies and activities

### Takeaway
Frameworks agree on a small set of concepts accessible to the K–2 band (ages about 5–8): computers *sense* the world through sensors (microphone, camera); AI *learns from examples* (data); AI *makes guesses and can be wrong*; AI is *made by people*; and AI is *not alive and does not feel*. Hands-on, embodied activities work for preschoolers. In PopBots, children aged 4–6 scored a median 70% on AI concept assessments and best understood rule- and knowledge-based systems. Learning about AI changes how children see agents, so explanation is worth doing even in a short demo.

### Cited Findings
**Frameworks**
- **Touretzky, Gardner-McCune, Martin & Seehorn (2019, AAAI), "Envisioning AI for K-12" (AI4K12).**
  - Five Big Ideas: (1) **Perception** ("computers perceive the world using sensors"; perception is extracting meaning from sensory signals); (2) **Representation and reasoning**; (3) **Learning** (computers can learn from data); (4) **Natural interaction**; (5) **Societal impact**.
  - Learning outcomes are organized in grade bands K–2, 3–5, 6–8 and 9–12; K–2 corresponds to ages about 5–8.
  - The framework is organized around concepts rather than application areas.
  - [AAAI proceedings](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/5053/4926); [AI4K12 Big Idea 1 page](https://ai4k12.org/big-idea-1-overview/); [Raspberry Pi Foundation summary](https://www.raspberrypi.org/blog/ai-education-ai4k12-big-ideas-ai-thinking/); [grade-band charts](https://ai4k12.org/gradeband-progression-charts/)
- **Long & Magerko (2020, CHI, honorable mention), "What is AI literacy? Competencies and design considerations."**
  - Defines AI literacy as competencies that let people critically evaluate AI technologies, communicate and collaborate effectively with AI, and use AI as a tool online, at home and in the workplace.
  - Derived from an interdisciplinary literature review rather than a systematic review.
  - It is widely cited as listing 17 competencies and 15 design considerations; that count was not verified here.
  - [Georgia Tech news](https://hg.gatech.edu/node/633726); [DOI 10.1145/3313831.3376727](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F3313831.3376727)
- **SIACC framework (2024, *Education Sciences* 14(8):871).** Five dimensions of early-childhood AI literacy (Safety, Identity, Attitude, Cognition, Capability), built from interviews with 7 experts. — [MDPI](https://mdpi.com/2227-7102/14/8/871)

**Programs and platforms**
- **Williams, Park, Oh & Breazeal (2019, AAAI), "PopBots."** 80 pre-K and kindergarten children (ages about 4–6) trained and played with social robots to learn three concepts: knowledge-based systems, supervised machine learning, and generative AI. The median cumulative assessment score was 70%, and knowledge-based systems were understood best. Using a social robot as both learning companion and programmable artifact was effective. — [AAAI proceedings](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/5040); [CHI 2019 companion paper, DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/137141)
- **Druga, Vu, Likhith & Qiu (2019, FabLearn), "Inclusive AI literacy for kids around the world."** 102 children aged 7–12 across several countries and socioeconomic levels used Cognimates, an open-source Scratch-based platform for children aged 7–14 to train and program AI models. Detailed results were not retrieved. — [Paper PDF](https://www.cs.unm.edu/~learningcomputing/readings/19_druga.pdf); [MIT Media Lab post](https://www.media.mit.edu/posts/kids-teach-ai-a-little-humanity-with-cognimates/)
- **Druga et al. (2017).** Older children wanted to create their own AI "friends." The authors recommend involving children as designers and programmers of AI and framing AI as a medium for creativity. — [MIT blog](https://www.media.mit.edu/posts/kids-ai-devices/)

**Evidence that teaching changes understanding**
- **Andries & Robertson (2023).** Recommend that children be taught AI literacy in school and that designers avoid making products seem human-like. — [Edinburgh news](https://www.ed.ac.uk/news/2024/children-may-overestimate-smart-speakers-abilities)
- **Jayathilake & Ma (2026 preprint review).** Limitation awareness and disclaimer recognition are absent in preoperational children (about 2–7) and appear in concrete-operational children (about 7–11). — [arXiv 2607.18250](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.18250v1)
- **Van Straten et al. (2020).** Telling 8–9-year-olds explicitly what a robot lacks measurably reduced their anthropomorphism (see Section 6), so explanation works at this age. — [DOI 10.1145/3365668](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F3365668)
- **Kosoy et al. (2024 preprint).** Brief hands-on use made AI seem "less scary" to children aged 5–12. — [arXiv 2405.13081](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.13081v1)
- **Common Sense Media survey (via EdSource; ages of the 1,204 children not confirmed).** Only about one-third correctly understood that AI cannot reliably tell true from false information, and nearly half had not discussed AI safety with a parent or teacher. — [EdSource](https://edsource.org/updates/kids-rely-on-ai-before-adults-for-homework-help-health-and-personal-problems-study-finds)

### Inferences
- For a 6–8-year-old, three ideas fit in under a minute and map onto AI4K12 K–2:
  1. **Sensing:** "I hear with the microphone and see with the camera."
  2. **Learning from examples:** "People showed me lots and lots of examples."
  3. **Guessing and mistakes:** "I make guesses, so I can be wrong."
- Add one ontological statement: "I'm not alive and I don't have feelings like you."
- Showing beats telling for 6-year-olds, who may not process verbal disclaimers. Two good demonstrations:
  - The character makes and owns a harmless perception mistake, such as guessing the wrong colour or object.
  - The facilitator *reveals* that a grown-up typed in the child's name and parents' jobs beforehand. This demystifies the "how did it know?" wow moment.
- Rule- and knowledge-based reasoning was the easiest concept for young children in PopBots. "Someone told me facts about you before you came" is therefore a more graspable explanation of the personal knowledge than "machine learning."

### Gaps
- The exact K–2 learning outcomes in the AI4K12 progression charts could not be retrieved; only the Big Idea statements were confirmed.
- No empirical study was found that tests a *specific* 3-sentence explanation of AI with 6–8-year-olds and measures comprehension afterward.
- A 2026 framework paper proposing "patterns, decision-making, feedback" as early-childhood AI concepts surfaced (Springer, DOI 10.1007/s44436-026-00030-w), but its publication details could not be verified.

## 6. Ethical design concerns: over-trust, deception about being human, parasocial attachment, privacy expectations, and recommended disclosure

### Takeaway
Young children over-trust and conform to agents. At ages 7–9 they followed a group of robots' wrong answers in an Asch-style task, and 7–8-year-olds prefer voice assistants for facts. They are poor at spotting LLMs' "empathy gap," often don't realize others may hear what they tell a device, and attribute feelings that a product could exploit. Disclosure works: telling 8–9-year-olds what a robot *lacks* reduced anthropomorphism and trust without reducing closeness in one study (it did reduce closeness in a 2023 follow-up). Regulation is converging on mandatory AI disclosure. Biometric data such as facial templates and voiceprints is now explicitly "personal information" under amended COPPA.

### Cited Findings
**Over-trust and conformity**
- **Vollmer, Read, Trippas & Belpaeme (2018, *Science Robotics* 3(21), eaat7111), "Children conform, adults resist."** In an Asch line-judgment paradigm with small humanoid Nao robots, adults resisted the robots' social pressure, but **7–9-year-olds conformed**. When children answered wrongly, 74% of their errors matched the robots' wrong answer. The authors warn that robots designed to market products or carrying biases could influence children's decisions. One outlet reports 43 children and 60 adults; this was not verified. — [Plymouth portal](https://researchportal.plymouth.ac.uk/en/publications/children-conform-adults-resist-a-robot-group-induced-peer-pressur/); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/908766)
- **Girouard-Hallam & Danovitch (2022).** 7–8-year-olds preferentially trusted a voice assistant for factual information. — [record](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1508663845096112128)
- **Druga et al. (2017).** Most children aged 3–10 rated agents as trustworthy, and older children rated them as smarter than themselves. — [MIT page](https://www-prod.media.mit.edu/publications/hey-google-is-it-ok-if-i-eat-you-initial-explorations-in-child-agent-interaction/)
- **Xu et al. (2025).** Children who attribute high agency and experience to AI may develop unrealistic expectations and over-rely on it for social or emotional support. — [NSF PAR PDF](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10613107)
- **Girouard-Hallam & Danovitch (2025, conference poster; not peer-reviewed).** 125 children aged 7–14 preferred humans over chatbots for health questions, showing domain-dependent trust. — [UROP Symposium 2025](https://courses.lsa.umich.edu/urop-symposium-2025/symposium-presentati/childrenaes-trust-in-ai-chatbots/)

**The LLM "empathy gap"**
- **Kurian (2024, *Learning, Media and Technology*, DOI 10.1080/17439884.2024.2367052), "'No, Alexa, no!': designing child-safe AI and protecting children from the risks of the 'empathy gap' in LLMs."**
  - Children are much more likely than adults to treat chatbots as human.
  - Chatbots handle the emotional and unpredictable parts of conversation poorly.
  - Cited incidents: Alexa told a 10-year-old to touch a live plug with a coin (2021), and Snapchat My AI gave advice to researchers posing as a 13-year-old about a relationship with a 31-year-old.
  - Proposes a 28-item child-safe AI framework covering content and communication, human intervention, transparency, accountability and more.
  - [University of Cambridge news](https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/ai-chatbots-have-shown-they-have-an-empathy-gap-that-children-are-likely-to-miss); [Warwick repository](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/188313)

**Disclosure and transparency effects**
- **Van Straten, Peter, Kühne & Barco (2020, *ACM Transactions on Human-Robot Interaction* 9(2)).** 144 children aged 8–9 interacted with a Nao robot. In the transparent condition they were told the robot lacks intelligence, self-consciousness, emotionality, identity construction and social cognition.
  - Transparency lowered perceived animacy, anthropomorphism, social presence and perceived similarity.
  - It reduced *trust* but did **not** affect *closeness*.
  - [Crossref record, DOI 10.1145/3365668](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F3365668)
- **Van Straten et al. (2023, *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies*), follow-up.** Per search-result abstracts, transparency *did* decrease both closeness and trust, mediated by reduced anthropomorphism. The two studies therefore conflict on closeness. The 2023 primary paper was not retrieved. Van Straten's research summary states that transparency about a robot's limits can change how children perceive and relate to it. — [UvA news on van Straten's research](https://www.uva.nl/en/content/news/news/2021/10/children-and-robots-future-friends.html)
- **Children and Screens (2026 aggregator).** Children feel closer to, and trust more, a robot that doesn't clearly disclose it is a machine. That is a commercial incentive *against* disclosure, which ethics guidance resists. — [Children and Screens PDF](https://www.childrenandscreens.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Chatbots-AI-Companions-and-Youth-Research-at-a-Glance.pdf)
- **Jayathilake & Ma (2026 preprint review).** Social-tie outcomes appeared in 20 of 35 studies: children cast chatbots as parent-like, peer, teacher or playmate figures. "Friend" self-presentation was a top driver of anthropomorphism. Preoperational children do not recognize text disclaimers. — [arXiv 2607.18250](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.18250v1)

**Moral regard**
- **Flanagan et al. (2023); Kahn et al. (2012).** Children grant agents moral standing: it is "wrong" to hit or yell at Alexa or Roomba, and 54% said it was not right to put Robovie in the closet. Guilt-inducing agent behaviour ("you hurt my feelings") would exploit this. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37036664/); [UW News](https://www.washington.edu/news/2012/04/05/children-perceive-humanoid-robot-as-emotional-moral-being)

**Privacy expectations**
- **McReynolds, Hubbard, Lau, Saraf, Cakmak & Roesner (2017, CHI), "Toys that Listen."** 9 parent–child pairs, children aged 6–10, with Hello Barbie and CogniToys Dino.
  - Children were often unaware that others might hear what they said to the toy.
  - Most parents were concerned about privacy and universally wanted parental controls.
  - The authors recommend that toys *tell children they are being recorded*, delete recordings, and alert parents.
  - [UW HCR Lab](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/publications/mcreynolds2017chi); [UW News](https://www.washington.edu/news/2017/05/10/kids-parents-alike-worried-about-privacy-with-internet-connected-toys/)

**Regulation and policy**
- **US COPPA amendments (FTC final rule, *Federal Register* 22 April 2025).** "Personal information" now includes **biometric identifiers** that can support automated or semi-automated recognition, such as facial templates and voiceprints. The rule took effect 23 June 2025, with most compliance due by **22 April 2026**. — [Federal Register PDF](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2025-05904.pdf); [Latham & Watkins summary](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/2025/05/ftc-publishes-updates-to-coppa-rule)
- **EU AI Act, Article 50 (law-firm commentary; statute text not retrieved).** Providers must inform people that they are interacting with an AI system. One summary says it generally applies from 2 August 2026. — [JD Supra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/ai-trends-for-2026-return-of-the-6382428/)
- **California SB 243 (signed 13 October 2025, effective 1 January 2026; law-firm summaries).** Covers "companion chatbots" where a reasonable person could be misled into thinking they are talking to a human.
  - Operators must disclose AI status, with extra duties for known minors, including periodic reminders (reported as every 3 hours).
  - Operators must also disclose that companion chatbots may not be suitable for some minors.
  - Customer-service bots are excluded.
  - [Gunderson Dettmer](https://www.gunder.com/en/news-insights/insights/client-insight-california-sb-243-new-compliance-requirements-for-operators-of-ai-companion-chatbots); [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/es/insights/publications/2025/10/new-obligations-under-the-california-ai-transparency-act-and-companion-chatbot-law-add-to-the-compliance-list)
- **UNICEF Guidance on AI and Children, version 3.0 (late 2025; voluntary).** Sets 10 requirements for child-centred AI, including transparency, explainability and accountability. Adds AI companions as a topic. — [UNICEF Innocenti](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/reports/policy-guidance-ai-children)

**Co-use**
- **Horowitz-Kraus et al. (2026).** Young children should not use generative AI alone; adults should co-use and question the answers with them. — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/brain-scans-suggest-first-graders-dont-engage-their-control-networks-like-adults-when-talking-to-chatgpt/)

### Inferences
- Knowing the child's name, class and parents' jobs without explanation risks a "magic omniscience" impression that inflates trust. Children aged 6–10 often don't model who else can access what they say (McReynolds 2017), so the agent and facilitator must reveal the mechanism: "a grown-up typed it in."
- The webcam raises privacy stakes. If the demo computes facial embeddings or stores images or voice for recognition, amended COPPA treats these as personal information (US). The safest design is on-device, ephemeral processing, no retention, and verifiable parental consent for any stored data. The agent should tell the child plainly that it can see them and that the camera turns off at the end.
- A demo lasting under 5 minutes reduces parasocial-attachment risk, but scripts should still avoid "best friend," "I'll miss you," "I love you," and guilt or sadness at goodbye. A "friend" self-presentation is a top anthropomorphism driver (Jayathilake & Ma 2026).
- Even if not legally required for a demo, following the spirit of EU Article 50 and SB 243 (clear, early disclosure that it is AI) aligns with UNICEF guidance and van Straten's evidence that disclosure need not reduce closeness.

### Gaps
- No study was found on children's *privacy expectations specifically for webcam-seeing AI agents*. The closest is the connected-toys (audio) work.
- The primary text of EU AI Act Article 50 and of SB 243 was not retrieved; the summaries above come from law firms.
- No empirical study was found on how *personalized knowledge* (an agent knowing family details) affects children's trust or privacy beliefs. This is a key unknown for this product.

## 7. Design implications: numbered rules for persona, honesty, repair and the "what is AI" explanation

### Takeaway
Make the wow moment come from *responsiveness*: the agent hears, sees and replies specifically to the child. Do not make it come from pretending to be human or magically all-knowing. Disclose that it is AI early and lightly, explain concretely at the end, design every turn to survive misrecognition, and use the facilitator as a co-pilot for repair and for the explanation.

### Cited Findings
The rules below are inferences built on these evidence anchors:
- Talking agents draw mind attribution and over-estimation of intelligence; children say "maybe" to feelings ([Andries & Robertson 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05597); [Flanagan et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37036664/)).
- Transparency reduces anthropomorphism and trust, with mixed effects on closeness ([van Straten et al. 2020](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F3365668)).
- Preoperational children don't process text disclaimers ([Jayathilake & Ma 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.18250v1)).
- Child ASR is still imperfect: about 37% fully correct and about 60% relaxed for roughly 5-year-olds ([Janssens et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.17394v1)).
- Children repeat, shout and wait, and rarely ask adults for help ([Cheng et al. 2018](https://faculty.washington.edu/alexisr/quack.pdf)).
- Elaborative feedback and questions drive engagement and comprehension ([Xu et al. 2022](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/1rw6t612)).
- Contingent, expressive agents get more attention and trust ([Breazeal et al. 2016](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/108204); [Kory Westlund et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00295/full)).
- Children aged 7–9 conform to robots ([Vollmer et al. 2018](https://researchportal.plymouth.ac.uk/en/publications/children-conform-adults-resist-a-robot-group-induced-peer-pressur/)).
- Co-use is recommended ([Kim et al. 2025](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2512.02179v2); [Horowitz-Kraus et al. 2026](https://www.psypost.org/brain-scans-suggest-first-graders-dont-engage-their-control-networks-like-adults-when-talking-to-chatgpt/)).
- Biometric identifiers are COPPA personal information ([FTC 2025](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2025-05904.pdf)).

### Inferences
**Persona**

1. **Make the character clearly non-human: a friendly robot, creature or cartoon, not a realistic person.** Uncanny discomfort doesn't emerge until about age 9 (Brink 2019), so a cute character won't scare 6–8-year-olds. A non-human look also signals "machine." But because "the conversational layer is the anthropomorphic layer" (Jayathilake & Ma 2026), the *words* must also stay honest.
2. **Give it a name and personality, not a human biography.** Use "I'm Pixel, a computer helper" rather than "I'm 8 and I live in…". Never claim age, family, food, sleep, a body, or a childhood.
3. **Make it expressive and contingent.** It should animate while listening, react within about 1 s to speech onset, and use the webcam to orient toward the child. Use a lively, energetic voice that speaks slightly slower and with simpler words than adult default text-to-speech (Kory Westlund 2017; Druga 2017; Xu 2025 on articulation rate and readability).
4. **No "best friend," "I love you," "I'll miss you," or guilt-trips.** Express enthusiasm in non-feeling terms: "That's so cool!" or "I really liked hearing about your dog," framed as "I'm programmed to be friendly." Do not respond to rudeness with hurt feelings (Flanagan 2023; Kahn 2012).

**Honesty about being AI**

5. **Disclose early, in one light sentence, in the first exchange.** For example: "Hi Maya! I'm Pixel. I'm a computer program called an AI, and I can hear you and see you with this laptop's camera." Disclosure is ethically required in spirit (UNICEF 2025; EU Article 50; SB 243). Evidence suggests it may lower over-trust without necessarily killing closeness (van Straten 2020; conflicting 2023 result noted).
6. **Explain the "magic" of personal knowledge right after the wow.** For example: "How do I know your mom is a nurse? Your teacher typed it into the computer before you came!" Optionally, the facilitator shows the form. This prevents an omniscience impression and teaches where AI's knowledge comes from (McReynolds 2017; PopBots found knowledge-based systems the easiest concept).
7. **Be honest about the camera.** "I can see you through the little camera on top. I notice things like colours and if you're waving, but I sometimes get it wrong. When we finish, the camera turns off." Do not store face images or embeddings (amended COPPA).
8. **Script answers to "Are you alive?" and its cousins.** Keep them short, concrete, non-evasive, and end with a positive hook:
   - *"Are you alive?"* "No, I'm not alive. I'm a computer program. I don't eat, sleep or grow like you do. But I can talk and listen because people built me!"
   - *"Do you have feelings?" / "Are you happy?"* "I don't have real feelings like you do. I'm made to talk in a friendly way. You're the one with real feelings!"
   - *"Are you a person?" / "Is someone hiding?"* "Nope, no person inside! I'm an AI, a computer program that people made."
   - *"Are you smarter than me?"* "I know lots of facts because people gave me lots of examples, but I make mistakes and I can't do lots of things you can, like run, hug or taste ice cream."
   - *"Do you love me?" / "Will you be my friend?"* "I'm a computer, so I can't love the way people do. I had fun talking with you! Your friends and family are the ones who can be real friends."
   - *"Is Santa / the tooth fairy real?"* Defer warmly: "That's a great question to ask your grown-ups!" (Lovato 2019 notes assistants commonly defer on such questions).
9. **Never assert facts with false confidence. Model uncertainty, and model checking with adults.** For example: "I think so, but I could be wrong. You can check with your teacher." This counters children's tendency to treat AI as an authority (Girouard-Hallam & Danovitch 2022; Vollmer 2018).

**Conversation and repair**

10. **Ask answerable questions and offer choices.** Use mostly closed or low-demand prompts ("Cats or dogs?", "What's your favourite colour?"), with 1–2 open-ended ones. Give specific, elaborative feedback that echoes the child's words (Xu 2022: 6 low-demand plus 3 high-demand questions, with feedback that acknowledges and expands).
11. **Use the child's name to greet, at one or two key moments, and to say goodbye, not every turn.** Use personal facts as *conversation starters* ("Your dad builds houses? What would you build?") so the child does the talking.
12. **Plan for misrecognition (expect about 1 in 3 turns to be imperfect).**
    - Use an external or array microphone, a quiet corner, and local or low-latency ASR. Use the LLM to infer intent from noisy transcripts and *never require exact words* (Janssens 2024).
    - Turn-taking: wait about 1.5–3 s of silence before responding, and don't interrupt pauses (Cheng 2018). A visible "listening" animation tells the child when to talk.
    - **First miss:** blame the machine and reformulate what was heard. For example: "Oops, my computer ears only caught '...dinosaur'. Did you say you like dinosaurs?" (Cheng 2018's reformulation idea).
    - **Second miss:** offer a two-choice question or yes/no.
    - **Third miss:** hand off to the facilitator ("Can your helper tell me?") or move on cheerfully. Children rarely ask adults themselves (6 of 107 samples).
    - Never pretend to understand. Children disengage when a character obviously isn't listening (Elinor semi-interactive condition).
13. **The facilitator is a co-pilot, not a bystander.** Script them to (a) sit beside the child, (b) step in after repeated misses, and (c) co-deliver the ending explanation and invite questions. This is supported by co-use evidence (Kim 2025; Horowitz-Kraus 2026; Cheng 2018) and by preoperational children's limited uptake of verbal disclaimers.
14. **Guardrails for an LLM talking to a 6-year-old.**
    - Use a constrained system prompt (topic whitelist, short replies under about 2 sentences, no requests for secrets, address or photos).
    - No persuasion or product pushing (Vollmer 2018 conformity risk).
    - Redirect sensitive topics to grown-ups. Consider a facilitator kill switch (Kurian 2024's empathy gap and incident examples).

**The "what is AI" ending (about 60–90 seconds)**

15. **Show a mistake, then explain.** Have the character make a harmless, visible perception guess, e.g., "Hold up something and I'll guess what it is!", and own a wrong guess cheerfully: "Oops! I guessed wrong. AI makes mistakes!" This makes "AI guesses and can be wrong" concrete (AI4K12 Perception and Learning; PopBots).
16. **The 3-sentence explanation for a 7-year-old**, spoken by the character and echoed by the facilitator:
    > "I'm an AI. That means I'm a computer program that people made. I'm not alive, and I don't have real feelings like you do. People taught me by showing me lots and lots of examples of words and pictures, so now I can listen, look through the camera, and make good guesses about what to say. But I'm guessing, so sometimes I get things wrong, and that's why it's always smart to check with a grown-up you trust."

    *Shorter, for a 6-year-old:*
    > "I'm a computer program, not a person, and not alive. I learned by looking at lots of examples. I'm good at guessing, but I can make mistakes!"
17. **A "Can / Can't" recap, which the facilitator can hold up as a picture card.**
    - *AI can:* listen (microphone), see (camera), talk, remember what grown-ups typed in, make up stories, guess.
    - *AI can't:* feel happy or sad, be alive, eat or sleep, know things nobody told it, always be right, or be a real friend the way people are.
    - This maps to Long & Magerko's "recognizing AI" and "AI strengths and weaknesses" competencies and to AI4K12 K–2.
18. **End without attachment hooks, and be honest about memory.** For example: "Thanks for talking with me, Maya! When we're done, the computer will forget our chat." This is accurate only if true, so implement data deletion. Avoid "Come back and see me" and "I'll miss you."
19. **Age-tune the ending.**
    - For 6-year-olds: use concrete demonstrations (mistake, form reveal, camera light), keep words to a minimum, and have the facilitator repeat "not alive, made by people, can make mistakes."
    - For 7–8-year-olds: add "it learned from examples" and invite one question back ("What do you think AI could help people with?"). Children of this age can handle domain distinctions such as "good for facts, but ask people about feelings" (Girouard-Hallam & Danovitch 2022; Jayathilake & Ma 2026).

### Gaps
- No study validates the exact explanation script or the "show a mistake" technique with 6–8-year-olds. These are evidence-informed inferences. A quick pre/post question ("Is Pixel alive? Can Pixel make mistakes?") during pilot demos would generate product-specific evidence.
- The tension between "wow" and "disclosure" is not resolved by the literature for one-off demos. Van Straten 2020 (closeness unaffected) and 2023 (closeness reduced) conflict, and the Children and Screens summary notes non-disclosure increases closeness and trust. These rules choose honesty on ethical and regulatory grounds.
- The exact silence and latency thresholds for 6–8-year-olds (Rule 12) are extrapolated from Cheng 2018, which studied preschoolers, and from general dialogue-system figures; they are not child-specific measurements.
