# Developmental Psychology of 6–8-Year-Olds for Designing a Spoken Conversation with an AI Character

Scope note: findings are for ages 6–8 where data exist. Where studies bin ages differently (e.g., 3–6, 4–11, 7–9, grade 1 = "nominally 6"), the bin is stated. Citations marked † are well-established papers cited with a DOI from the existing literature but not re-fetched in this research session; the report writer should spot-check these before publication. Everything else was confirmed through search or fetch in this session (often via abstract or secondary summary, which is noted).

---

## 1. Cognitive stage: what 6–8-year-olds can and cannot understand, and how to explain "what AI is"

### Takeaway
6–8-year-olds are moving from preoperational to concrete-operational thinking. They reason well about concrete, visible, explained cause-and-effect, but they still take metaphors literally, often over-attribute minds to talking devices, and are unsure whether voice AIs have feelings. Explain AI with a concrete demo, an explicit "who told me" source, and a plain contrast with magic, not with metaphors.

### Cited Findings
**Piagetian stage and its caveats**
- Piaget placed the preoperational stage at about 2–7 years and the concrete-operational stage at about 7–11/12. In the concrete stage children reason logically about concrete objects, for example conservation and reversibility, but not yet about abstract or hypothetical propositions. — [Britannica: Jean Piaget](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jean-Piaget) †
- The stage ages are approximate. Children's reasoning shifts with their competence: those who had *just* acquired conservation found conservation-violating jokes funniest, for example a pizza cut into 6 slices "because I can't eat 8" (the cognitive-congruency principle). — McGhee 1976, *Child Development* 47:420–426, [doi:10.2307/1128797](https://doi.org/10.2307/1128797) †

**Theory of mind and understanding where knowledge comes from**
- First-order false-belief understanding emerges around age 4, according to a meta-analysis of 178 studies. — Wellman, Cross & Watson 2001, *Child Development*, [doi:10.1111/1467-8624.00304](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8624.00304) †
- Second-order belief reasoning ("John thinks that Mary thinks…") emerges around 6–7 years. — Perner & Wimmer 1985, *J. Experimental Child Psychology* 39:437–471, [doi:10.1016/0022-0965(85)90051-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-0965(85)90051-7) †
- Preschoolers already link informational access ("seeing/being told") to knowing. By 6–8, children can follow an explanation such as "a grown-up told me your name". — Wimmer, Hogrefe & Perner 1988, *Child Development* 59:386–396, [doi:10.2307/1130318](https://doi.org/10.2307/1130318) †

**Metaphor, analogy and hypotheticals**
- Young children interpret psychological metaphors literally or "magically" ("the prison guard was a hard rock" taken to mean he became a rock). In later summaries of this work, "primitive-metaphoric" readings are frequent at about 8, and genuine metaphoric interpretation appears around 10. — Winner, Rosenstiel & Gardner 1976, *Developmental Psychology* 12:289–297, summarized in [Ortony et al. 1980 critique](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~ortony/Andrew_Ortony_files/1980%20-%20Issues%20in%20measurement.pdf) and [Vosniadou et al. 1984 (Illinois)](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/17913/bitstreams/64210/data.pdf)
  - Caveat: critics note that the 1976 metaphors came with almost no context. Preschoolers do understand simple perceptual metaphors in supportive contexts (same sources).
- Even preschoolers can solve problems by analogy when the analog is concrete and the mapping is made explicit (the "genie" story analog). Spontaneous, abstract mapping is much weaker in young children. — Holyoak, Junn & Billman 1984, *Child Development*, [PDF](https://reasoninglab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/273/2021/04/HolyoakJunnBillman1984.pdf)
- Children aged 4–6 reason correctly from counterfactual premises when the premises are framed as make-believe or "imagine…" play. — Dias & Harris 1988, *British J. Developmental Psychology* 6:207–221, [doi:10.1111/j.2044-835X.1988.tb01095.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-835X.1988.tb01095.x) †

**Magic vs. explanation**
- Between ages 4 and 8, children increasingly explain unusual events physically rather than magically. Magical explanations are used mainly when children lack a physical explanation. — Phelps & Woolley 1994, *Developmental Psychology* 30:385–394, [doi:10.1037/0012-1649.30.3.385](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.30.3.385) †

**How children this age think about AI, voice assistants and robots**
- Flanagan, Wong & Kushnir (2023) studied N = 127 children aged 4–11 (US science museum, 75% White). They credited Alexa with more thinking and emotion than Roomba, but judged neither able to feel pain or tickling. Attributions of agency declined with age. Children of all ages said it was wrong to hit or yell at the devices. — *Developmental Psychology* 59(6), [doi:10.1037/dev0001524](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0001524); [Duke summary](https://today.duke.edu/2023/04/kids-judge-alexa-smarter-roomba-say-both-deserve-kindness)
- Andries & Robertson (2023) studied N = 166 children aged 6–11 in Scotland with smart speakers at home. Most overestimated the assistants' intelligence, and many answered "maybe" when asked whether the assistant has feelings or makes its own decisions. The authors recommend that designers avoid misleading human-likeness. — *Computers & Education: AI*, [arXiv:2305.05597](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05597); [Edinburgh news](https://edinburghuni-newsroom.prgloo.com/news/children-may-overestimate-smart-speakers-abilities-study-finds)
- Girouard-Hallam & Danovitch (2022) compared children aged 4–5 and 7–8. Older children increasingly went to a voice assistant for facts and to a human for personal information. — *Developmental Psychology* 58(4):646–661, [doi:10.1037/dev0001318](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0001318) ([index](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1508663845096112128))
- In a 240-child sample aged 3–18 (plus a follow-up study), children up to about age 8 rated human-like and machine-like robots as equally "not very creepy". The uncanny-valley creepiness for human-like robots appeared from about age 9 and grew with age. — Brink, Gray & Wellman 2019, *Child Development* 90(4):1202–1214, [doi:10.1111/cdev.12999](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12999) ([accepted manuscript](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/handle/2027.42/150519/cdev12999_am.pdf?sequence=1))
- Children treat personified robots as a new category, neither alive nor merely objects ("behaving as if"). — Severson & Carlson 2010, *Neural Networks* 23:1099–1103, [doi:10.1016/j.neunet.2010.08.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neunet.2010.08.014) †
- Young children can learn basic AI concepts through hands-on activities. With the PopBots platform, preschoolers trained social robots and reached a median 70% on the cumulative assessment, understanding knowledge-based systems best. How much they learned was related to how they perceived robots, though summaries disagree on the direction. — Williams, Park & Breazeal 2019, *CHI*, [doi:10.1145/3290605.3300677](https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300677); [MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/a-is-for-ai/)

### Inferences
- A single sentence like "AI is like a brain made of maths" will likely be heard literally. Use concrete, perceptual statements tied to what just happened ("A grown-up typed your name into this computer before you came. The computer used it to talk to you.").
- At 6–8, an explicit source for personal knowledge is developmentally easy to understand (informational access) and fits older children's expectation that personal facts come from humans. Saying where the knowledge came from should turn "spooky" into "clever".
- Children of this age are unlikely to be creeped out by the character's look (pre-uncanny-valley), but they are prone to over-attribute feelings and intelligence. An honest close ("I'm a computer program. I don't have feelings like you do.") is warranted, given the Andries & Robertson recommendation.
- "Imagine if…" framing works for hypotheticals at this age. Bare abstract conditionals ("If an AI were trained on…") do not.
- A "magic, then reveal" arc fits the 4–8 shift from magical to physical explanation. Children at 6–8 are ready to hear "it's not magic, it's a computer".

### Gaps
- I found no study that tested specific "what is AI" explanation scripts with 6–8-year-olds in a single 5-minute session. The PopBots work is with preschoolers over multiple activities.
- Xu & Warschauer 2020 (CHI, [doi:10.1145/3313831.3376416](https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376416)) on children's perceptions of conversational agents was confirmed bibliographically, but its findings could not be retrieved.
- There are no data on whether an AI that knows parents' names and jobs is experienced by 6–8-year-olds as delightful or unsettling. This is an open empirical question.

## 2. Attention span and working memory

### Takeaway
The "N minutes per year of age" rule has no identifiable primary source. Sustained attention improves fastest between 5–6 and 8–9 and breaks down under heavier load. Working memory at 6–7 holds about 2–2.5 chunks versus 3–4 in adults. Design for one idea per utterance and frequent activity changes.

### Cited Findings
**Myth check: "attention span = 2–5 minutes per year of age"**
- A search for the origin of the rule found only parenting and education blogs. They disagree on the multiplier (2–3 min, 2–5 min, or "age + 1") and cite no primary study. One attributes it to a university Cooperative Extension sheet. — [EarlyYears.tv](https://www.earlyyears.tv/attention-span-development/), [Tes](https://www.tes.com/news/how-bring-learning-sharper-focus), [Edutopia](https://edutopia.org/article/are-student-attention-spans-really-shrinking). **Verdict: unsupported heuristic. Do not cite it as evidence.**
- A related adult myth, the "10-minute lecture attention span", was traced to weak or misread evidence. — Bradbury 2016, *Advances in Physiology Education*, [doi:10.1152/advan.00109.2016](https://doi.org/10.1152/advan.00109.2016) †
- In toddlers and preschoolers, focused attention during free play depends heavily on the activity and the materials, not just on age. — Ruff & Lawson 1990, *Developmental Psychology* 26:85–93, [doi:10.1037/0012-1649.26.1.85](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.26.1.85) †

**Sustained attention development**
- Betts et al. (2006) tested N = 57 children aged 5–12 on CogState and TEA-Ch Score! tasks. Sustained attention improved rapidly between 5–6 and 8–9, then plateaued (with minor gains to about 10). Performance was poorer on high-load tasks at every age. — Betts, McKay, Maruff & Anderson 2006, *Child Neuropsychology* 12:205–221, [doi:10.1080/09297040500488522](https://doi.org/10.1080/09297040500488522) ([abstract](https://www.cogstate.com/publication/the-development-of-sustained-attention-in-children-the-effect-of-age-and-task-load/))
- A follow-up on the same sample found rapid improvements between 5 and 8, with more moderate (speed) gains between 9 and 12. — McKay et al. 2010, *Educational & Developmental Psychologist*, [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/educational-and-developmental-psychologist/article/an-examination-of-developmental-trajectories-for-attention-skills-in-children/F82EAE0934523F45ADCFFE0372F37E7A)
- In a kindergarten lab classroom, heavily decorated walls increased off-task behaviour (about 38.6% of time vs about 28.4% in a sparse room) and reduced learning. — Fisher, Godwin & Seltman 2014, *Psychological Science*, [doi:10.1177/0956797614533801](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614533801) †

**Working memory**
- Cowan's 2016 review gives two estimates:
  - A sentence-chunk task: about 2.5 chunks in first graders (≈7–8 years in that study) vs about 3.5 in adults (Gilchrist, Cowan & Naveh-Benjamin 2009).
  - Visual arrays: about 2–2.5 items for preschoolers and early-elementary children vs about 3–4 in adults.
  - Source: Cowan 2016, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, [PMC4800832](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4800832/)
- Digit span rose about 24% over the elementary-school years. Much of that gain reflects more efficient chunking rather than raw capacity (Dempster 1978, as summarized in Cowan 2016). — [PMC4800832](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4800832/)
- Working memory components show a stable structure and steady linear growth from 4 to 15 years. — Gathercole, Pickering, Ambridge & Wearing 2004, *Developmental Psychology* 40:177–190, [doi:10.1037/0012-1649.40.2.177](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.40.2.177) †
- Following spoken multi-step instructions (children aged 7–9) is closely linked to verbal complex working memory. Recall improved when children physically performed the actions while hearing them or at recall. Simply repeating the instructions aloud did not help. — Jaroslawska, Gathercole, Allen & Holmes 2016, *Memory & Cognition*, [PMC5085979](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5085979)

### Inferences
- With about 2.5 chunks of capacity, one AI turn should carry one new idea plus at most one question. Two questions in a row, or "first X, then Y, then tell me Z", will overload many 6-year-olds.
- Rapid attention development across 5–9 means the 6-year-olds will need noticeably more scaffolding (shorter segments, more prompts) than the 8-year-olds. Consider an age-adaptive script keyed off the grade field.
- Clean visual design matters. A busy animated background competes for limited attention (Fisher et al.).
- Asking the child to *do* something (wave, point, show a thumbs-up) improves retention of instructions (Jaroslawska) and also gives shy children a non-verbal way to take part.

### Gaps
- No rigorous norm was found for how long a 6–8-year-old stays engaged in one conversational activity. Any specific "segment length" is a design inference, not a finding.
- No verified forward digit-span norms by single year of age were retrieved (test-manual norms such as WISC are proprietary).

## 3. Language: vocabulary, utterance length, comprehension, speech rate, children's speech, turn-taking and response latency

### Takeaway
6–8-year-olds understand far more words than they produce, speak in utterances of about 5–6 morphemes, and still mispronounce late sounds (/θ/, /ð/, /r/). They respond much more slowly than adults (adults: about 0–200 ms gaps; young children: often over 1 s, slower for complex answers). They need louder speech relative to background noise than adults do. Wait time of 3 s or more reliably increases children's responses in classrooms.

### Cited Findings
**Vocabulary**
- Anglin's monograph on 6–10-year-olds found that recognition vocabulary grows rapidly, with an increasing share of complex words worked out through morphology. Secondary sources cite about 1,100–1,200 root words and about 3,500 derived words learned per year between grades 1 and 5. — Anglin 1993, *Monographs of the SRCD* 58(10), [doi:10.2307/1166112](https://doi.org/10.2307/1166112); figures via [Biemiller summary](https://www.scribd.com/document/45815928/Biemiller-Teaching-Vocab) and [Reading vocabulary-size review](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/29879/2/vocabulary%20size%20revisited_JM_JTD.pdf)
  - The widely repeated totals (about 10,000 words at grade 1, about 20,000 at grade 3, about 40,000 at grade 5) could not be confirmed in this session. Treat them as approximate.

**Mean length of utterance**
- Rice et al. (2010) report MLU norms in 6-month bins from 2;6 to 9;0 (N = 306, 136 typically developing). MLU in words and morphemes rises with age. — *JSLHR* 53:333–349, [doi:10.1044/1092-4388(2009/08-0183)](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1092-4388(2009/08-0183)); [PMC2849178](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2849178)
  - A clinic summary of these norms gives about 5.5–5.7 morphemes at 7;6–8;11. This figure comes from a secondary source and has not been checked against the paper's tables. — [Emerge Pediatric Therapy](https://www.emergepediatrictherapy.com/wp-content/uploads/transferred/13FebMeanLengthUtterance.pdf)

**Speech rate and comprehension**
- Slowing speech did not improve sentence comprehension in typically developing school-age children, though it helped children with language impairment (Montgomery 2004, as summarized). — [Lund University paper](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/2053614/4279978.pdf)
- Faster speech slowed sentence processing in both normal-hearing and deaf/hard-of-hearing children. — Abrahamse et al. 2025, *JSLHR*, [doi:10.1044/2025_JSLHR-24-00108](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2025_JSLHR-24-00108)
- Children's own articulation rate increases through middle childhood, is generally adult-like by about age 10, and varies widely between children. — Mahr et al. 2021, *JSLHR*, [doi:10.1044/2021_JSLHR-21-00206](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2021_JSLHR-21-00206)

**Speech in noise**
- Bradley & Sato tested real classrooms in grades 1, 3 and 6 (nominally 6, 8 and 11 years). For 95% correct speech intelligibility, grade 1 needed about +15.5 dB signal-to-noise ratio, grade 3 about +12.5 dB and grade 6 about +8.5 dB. They concluded that +15 dB is not adequate for the youngest children; a secondary source cites about +20 dB as preferable for grade 1. — Bradley & Sato 2008, *JASA*; figures via [Acoustical Surfaces summary of the 2004 ICA paper](https://www.acousticalsurfaces.com/?p=1770) and [J. Canadian Acoustics](https://jcaa.caa-aca.ca/index.php/jcaa/article/download/1610/1357)

**How children's speech differs**
- A review of 15 studies (18,907 US children) found that most English consonants are acquired by 5;0. Liquids (/l/, /ɹ/) come by about 5;11, all fricatives by about 6;11, and /θ/ (as in "thing") is the last, at 6;0–6;11 (90% criterion). — Crowe & McLeod 2020, *AJSLP* 29(4), [doi:10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168)
- Children's speech has longer segment durations and much greater within-speaker variability in timing and spectra than adult speech. It converges to adult values only in early adolescence. — Lee, Potamianos & Narayanan 1999, *JASA* 105:1455–1468, [doi:10.1121/1.426686](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.426686) †
- Normal disfluency includes filled pauses, revisions and even some repetitions. Filled pauses are the most frequent type. In French-speaking 4–5-year-olds, total disfluencies were about 9.6–11.6 per 100 words. — [Leclercq, Suaire & Moyse 2017 (preprint)](https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/213815/1/Leclercq%20Suaire%20%26%20Moyse%202017_CLP_preprint.pdf); preschool English data in [Rescorla/TalkBank doc](https://talkbank.org/childes/access/Clinical-Eng/0docs/Rescorla.pdf). No solid school-age (6–8) disfluency norms were found.

**Turn-taking and response latency**
- Across 10 languages, adult turn transitions cluster around 0–200 ms. — Stivers et al. 2009, *PNAS*, [doi:10.1073/pnas.0903616106](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903616106) †
- Children answer questions more slowly than adults and reach adult timing only "several years later". Simple answers start faster than complex ones; this was studied at 1;8–3;5. — Casillas, Bobb & Clark 2016, *J. Child Language* 43:1310–1337, [doi:10.1017/S0305000915000689](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0305000915000689/type/journal_article)
- Corpus work reports median gaps above 600 ms in toddlers and preschoolers, and above 1,000 ms even in 5-year-olds (secondary summary of a Gambi et al. preprint). Preschoolers leave shorter gaps when they can predict how a question will end. — Lindsay, Gambi & Rabagliati 2019, *Psychological Science*, [doi:10.1177/0956797618822802](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797618822802/)
- A meta-analysis of 26 studies (429 infants) found that infant responses take about 1 s. The adult's own pause length strongly predicts the child's: each 1-s increase in adult latency goes with more than a 1-s increase in infant latency. This is infant data and may not carry over to 6–8-year-olds. — Nguyen, Versyp, Cox & Fusaroli 2022, *Child Development* 93(4):1181–1200, [preprint](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.31234/osf.io/3bak6)
- In classrooms, teachers typically wait under 1 s after a question (about 0.9 s average in Rowe's recordings). Rowe reported a threshold at about 2.7–3 s: above it, response length rose 300–700%, failures to respond fell, and unsolicited appropriate responses increased. The length figures are as repeated in secondary sources. — Rowe 1986, *J. Teacher Education* 37(1):43–50, [doi:10.1177/002248718603700110](https://doi.org/10.1177/002248718603700110) †; [Faculty Focus summary](https://www.facultyfocus.com/articles/effective-teaching-strategies/student-learning-in-3-seconds/)

**Children's repair with voice interfaces**
- Cheng et al. (2018) gave 14 preschoolers a deliberately broken voice game and recorded 107 failed attempts. Children repaired breakdowns by repeating (79% of children), speaking louder, or rephrasing. They persisted without visible frustration more than 75% of the time. — Cheng, Yen, Chen, Chen & Hiniker 2018, *IDC '18*; [UW News](https://www.washington.edu/news/tag/yi-cheng), [Quartz](https://qz.com/1352272/when-alexa-and-other-smart-speakers-misunderstand-little-kids)

### Inferences
- Pause length: adult-tuned end-of-turn detection (around 500–700 ms of silence) will cut 6–8-year-olds off mid-thought, because they take longer to start and pause more inside utterances. Tolerate about 1.5 s of mid-utterance silence before closing the child's turn, and wait 3–5 s or more after a question before any re-prompt. This combines the latency data, Rowe's wait-time threshold and the variability data. Because the child's latency tracks the partner's (Nguyen et al.), the AI should also not answer instantly in a rapid-fire style.
- Sentence length: the AI's sentences can be slightly longer than the child's own (about 6–12 words), but should be simple in structure (subject–verb–object, no embedded clauses). Speak at a natural, unhurried rate; slowing down further brings no gain for typical children (Montgomery).
- Audio: in a noisy school or event space, a laptop speaker is unlikely to reach +15–20 dB above background noise. Use an external speaker, a quieter corner, or a headset.
- Speech recognition: expect /r/, /th/ and /l/ substitutions in 6-year-olds, and Indian-English phonology (see Section 7). The AI should infer meaning generously rather than ask the child to repeat more than once. Children's natural repair is to repeat louder, which will not fix a recognition error, so the AI should offer a choice question instead.

### Gaps
- No direct measurement of conversational gap duration for typical 6–8-year-olds was found. The ">1 s at age 5" figure is from a secondary snippet.
- No study was found on the TTS speaking rate that 6–8-year-olds prefer.
- Loban-style words-per-communication-unit norms for grades 1–3 were not retrieved.

## 4. Social-emotional: shyness, warming up, own name and self-reference, parents' jobs

### Takeaway
About 15–20% of young children are temperamentally inhibited with unfamiliar people, and a few (about 0.7% in early grades) are selectively mute. Rapport built through open-ended, non-evaluative invitations about the child's own experience produces fuller and more accurate talk than direct question-and-answer rapport. A child's own name reliably captures neural attention at around age 8. Self-referenced material is remembered better. There is almost no research on talking with children about their parents' jobs.

### Cited Findings
**Behavioural inhibition and selective mutism**
- About 15–20% of children can be classified as behaviourally inhibited in early childhood, meaning wary or fearful in response to unfamiliar people and situations. Some of Kagan's work gives 10–15% depending on sample and criteria. — [Chronis-Tuscano et al. 2009, JAACAP](https://catlabpsu.com/s/Chronis-Tuscano-et-al-2009.pdf); [Frontiers 2023 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1193915/pdf); Kagan, Reznick & Snidman 1988, *Science* 240:167–171, [doi:10.1126/science.3353713](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.3353713) †
- Selective mutism prevalence was about 0.71% among kindergarten–2nd grade children in a school-based sample. — Bergman, Piacentini & McCracken 2002, *JAACAP* 41:938–946, [doi:10.1097/00004583-200208000-00012](https://doi.org/10.1097/00004583-200208000-00012) †

**Building rapport and warming up**
- In a staged-event study, children given open-ended rapport building ("tell me about…") gave more accurate later reports, after both short and long delays, than children given direct-question rapport. — Roberts, Lamb & Sternberg 2004, *Applied Cognitive Psychology*, [doi:10.1002/acp.1004](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1004) †; summarized in [NCAC rapport bibliography](https://files.calio.org/BIBS/rapport-building-bib1.pdf)
- A meta-analysis of 35 studies found a medium overall effect of rapport practices on children's disclosure, with support for open-ended questions (d ≈ 0.55). — [Meta-analysis PDF (CALiO/NCAC)](https://files.calio.org/Meta-analysis%20of%20the%20effects%20of%20two%20interviewing%20practices%20on%20children%e2%80%99s%20disclosures%20of%20sensitive%20information-%20Rapport%20practices%20and%20question%20type.pdf)
- "Narrative practice" early in an interview increased children's informative answers to open-ended prompts later. Rapport phases built on open-ended questions, back-channels ("mm-hm", "wow") and use of the child's name help train children to narrate. — [NCAC narrative-practice summary](https://files.calio.org/Other%20NCAC%20pubs/narrative-practice-rev.2015.pdf); [NSF-hosted review](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10547980)
- A 2025 study with children aged 5–8 found that open-ended rapport helped younger children's free recall. — reported in the [UTS repository result](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/196889/2/2146888.pdf) (details not verified)
- Children aged 5–8 avert their gaze more when thinking about harder questions. Looking away while thinking helps them answer rather than signalling disengagement. — Doherty-Sneddon et al. 2002, *Developmental Psychology* 38:438–445, [doi:10.1037/0012-1649.38.3.438](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.38.3.438) †

**Yes/no questions and acquiescence**
- Children aged about 5–9 frequently answer nonsensical yes/no questions ("Is red heavier than yellow?") with "yes" or "no" rather than "I don't know". They do say "I don't know" to nonsensical wh-questions. — Waterman, Blades & Spencer 2000, *British J. Developmental Psychology* 18:211–225, [doi:10.1348/026151000165652](https://doi.org/10.1348/026151000165652) †

**Own name and self-reference**
- In an ERP study of children with a mean age of 7.85 years, the parietal P300 attention response was larger for the child's own name, and also for a familiar other's name, than for unfamiliar names. — Key et al., [PMC4993658](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4993658/)
- In 7–13-year-olds, P3 amplitude was larger for the child's own name than for a close other's name, and the self-specific effect was stronger in older children. — [Erasmus MC abstract](https://erasmusmc.mijn.bsl.nl/intact-neural-responding-to-hearing-one-s-own-name-in-children-w/50437184)
- In an ownership paradigm with 4–6-year-olds (N = 64), toys designated "mine" were remembered better than toys designated "yours". The advantage decreased with age. — Cunningham, Vergunst, Macrae & Turk 2013, *British J. Developmental Psychology*, [Bristol record](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/exploring-early-self-referential-memory-effects-through-ownership/)
- Self-referential encoding improved 7–9-year-olds' spelling (Turk et al. 2015, "Selfish learning", *Learning and Instruction*), and 3-year-olds learned self-referenced novel words better. — summarized in Axelsson et al. 2018, *Frontiers in Psychology*, [PMC6005863](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6005863)
- Cordova & Lepper (1996) studied upper-elementary children (grades 4–5). Personalizing a maths game with the child's name, friends and favourite things, combined with offering choices, increased intrinsic motivation, engagement, depth of learning and perceived competence. — *J. Educational Psychology* 88:715–730, [Kellogg record](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/1996/intrinsic-motivation-and-the-process-of-learning-beneficial-effects)

**Parents' jobs as a topic**
- The evidence is thin. Children form intuitive notions of occupations early. Their knowledge and aspirations are anchored in family members' jobs. Around 6–8, children start sorting occupations by gender, often based on their parents' jobs. — [Frontiers 2021](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.730859/full); [ASEE paper](https://peer.asee.org/28376.pdf); [Wellesley Centers 1999 (older children, N = 25)](https://www.wcwonline.org/Publications-by-title/childrens-perception-of-their-parents-work)

### Inferences
- In a group of 30 demo children, expect about 5–6 to be noticeably inhibited and possibly one who is nearly silent. The script needs a no-pressure path: the AI talks first, uses non-verbal responses (wave, thumbs-up, point), and the facilitator is near the child.
- Warm-up should be open-ended but easy, and about the child's own world ("Tell me about your favourite game"). Avoid evaluative quiz questions. Back-channels ("Wow!", "Mm-hm!") and the child's name support narration.
- Don't treat the child looking away from the webcam as disengagement or "not listening". It is often thinking.
- Avoid "Do you understand?" or "Is that OK?" as checks, because children this age say "yes" by default. Use teach-back ("Can you tell your mum what I am?") or a two-option question.
- Use the child's name at a few key moments to capture attention and signal "I see you". Overuse probably weakens the effect. This is an inference; no study of name-frequency effects was found.
- Parents' jobs fit the self-reference and personalization effects (the child is the expert on their family). Asking the child to *teach* the AI about the job gives them competence and autonomy (see Section 5). Facilitators must vet the data for sensitive situations (job loss, separated or deceased parents).

### Gaps
- No study of children's enjoyment of talking about parents' occupations at 6–8 was found.
- Kucirkova et al.'s work on personalized story apps and books increasing young children's talk is a likely relevant source, but was not verified in this session.
- No study was found on how quickly inhibited 6–8-year-olds warm up to an on-screen character or voice agent compared with a human stranger.

## 5. Motivation and praise

### Takeaway
Experiments around this age band, at 4–6 years and 10–12 years, show that trait or "person" praise ("you're so smart", "you're a good drawer") makes children more helpless after setbacks, and at 3–5 more likely to cheat, than process praise. Effect sizes in the broader mindset literature are small, and a large replication failed. Specific, sincere, non-inflated process praise is a cheap best practice, not a guaranteed motivator. Tangible rewards undermine children's intrinsic motivation. Choice and personalization raise it.

### Cited Findings
**Person vs. process praise**
- In a series of studies with fifth graders (about 10–12 years), praise for intelligence led to more fixed-ability beliefs, more performance goals, less persistence and enjoyment, and worse performance after failure than praise for effort. — Mueller & Dweck 1998, *JPSP* 75:33–52, [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.75.1.33](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.75.1.33) †
- In role-play studies with 5–6-year-olds (Study 1, N = 67), person praise and criticism led to more "helpless" responses after a later setback, including self-blame, than process praise and criticism. — Kamins & Dweck 1999, *Developmental Psychology* 35:835–847, [PubMed 10380873](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10380873/)
- With 4-year-olds, generic praise ("You are a good drawer") produced worse responses to later mistakes than non-generic praise ("You did a good job drawing"). Children praised non-generically more often proposed ways to fix their drawings. — Cimpian, Arce, Markman & Dweck 2007, *Psychological Science* 18:314–316; [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-praise-children)
- Even small amounts of generic ("you're so smart") praise reduced persistence. Mixing praise types was also examined. — Zentall & Morris 2010, *J. Experimental Child Psychology*, [PubMed 20570281](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20570281/)
- In a study of 300 Chinese 3- and 5-year-olds, children praised for being "smart" peeked in a guessing game nearly 60% of the time, compared with just over 40% after performance praise or no praise. — Zhao, Heyman, Chen & Lee 2017, *Psychological Science*, [doi:10.1177/0956797617721529](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617721529); [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/praising-kids-smarts-may-lead-to-cheating.html)
- In a longitudinal study (N = 53), parents' process praise at 14–38 months predicted children's incremental ("ability can grow") beliefs at 7–8 years. Person praise did not predict fixed beliefs. — Gunderson et al. 2013, *Child Development* 84:1526–1541, [doi:10.1111/cdev.12064](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12064); [PMC3655123](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655123)

**Replications and caveats**
- Li & Bates (2019) ran three studies with 624 Chinese children aged 9–13 and largely failed to replicate Mueller & Dweck. Effects appeared on none of the 8 motivation and attribution measures, with only a marginal (p = .049) effect on one post-failure test. Dweck & Yeager disputed the replication's fidelity. — [Westminster record](https://westminsterresearch.westminster.ac.uk/item/v62v1/you-can-t-change-your-basic-ability-but-you-work-at-things-and-that-s-how-we-get-hard-things-done-testing-the-role-of-growth-mindset-on-response-to-setbacks-educational-attainment-and-cognitive); [replication PDF](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Mindset/Mindset%20replication.pdf)
- Meta-analyses found a weak mindset–achievement association (r ≈ .10) and very small intervention effects (d ≈ 0.08). — Sisk et al. 2018, *Psychological Science*, [doi:10.1177/0956797617739704](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617739704) †
- One critique notes that the person-vs-process conclusions rest heavily on two studies from one lab, both lacking a no-praise control. — [Keele repository review](https://keele-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/453043)
- Inflated praise ("incredibly beautiful!") led children aged 8–12 with low self-esteem to avoid challenges. — Brummelman et al. 2014, *Psychological Science* 25:728–735, [doi:10.1177/0956797613514251](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613514251) †
- Younger children tend to take praise at face value. Older children (about 11–12) begin to infer *low* ability from praise for easy successes. — Barker & Graham 1987, *J. Educational Psychology* 79:62–66, [doi:10.1037/0022-0663.79.1.62](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.79.1.62) †
- A review concluded that praise supports intrinsic motivation when it is sincere and specific, attributes success to controllable factors, supports autonomy, conveys attainable standards, and avoids social comparison. — Henderlong & Lepper 2002, *Psychological Bulletin* 128:774–795, [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.128.5.774](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.128.5.774) †

**Intrinsic motivation and Self-Determination Theory**
- A meta-analysis of 128 experiments found that tangible rewards were more detrimental to intrinsic motivation for children than for college students. Verbal rewards (positive feedback) were less enhancing for children than for college students. — Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, *Psychological Bulletin* 125:627–668, [PDF](https://home.ubalt.edu/ntygmitc/642/Articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)
- Classic "overjustification" study: preschoolers who expected a reward for drawing later drew less in free time. — Lepper, Greene & Nisbett 1973, *JPSP* 28:129–137, [doi:10.1037/h0035519](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0035519) †
- Self-Determination Theory says intrinsic motivation is supported by autonomy, competence and relatedness. — Ryan & Deci 2000, *American Psychologist* 55:68–78, [doi:10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68) †
- Personalization plus choice had the largest motivational effect in Cordova & Lepper 1996. — [Kellogg record](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/1996/intrinsic-motivation-and-the-process-of-learning-beneficial-effects)

### Inferences
- In a 5-minute demo, praise should name *what the child did* ("You told me three things about your cat!", "You thought really hard about that!"), not *what the child is* ("You're so smart / so clever / a genius"). Keep it warm but not inflated, and vary it.
- A short demo cannot change mindset. The practical goal is to avoid harm (no trait labels, no comparison with other children) and to make the child feel competent.
- Do not promise stickers or prizes for talking. A thank-you or souvenir given unconditionally at the end is fine. "Talk and you'll get a sticker" risks the overjustification effect.
- Autonomy: give the child 2–3 choices ("Should I tell you a joke or show you a trick?"). Competence: ask questions the child is certain to be able to answer. Relatedness: warmth and use of their name and family.

### Gaps
- No well-powered experiment tests person vs. process praise specifically in 6–8-year-olds. The age band is bracketed by studies at 3–6 and 9–13.
- No study was found on praise *from an AI or robot* versus from a human in this age group.

## 6. Humor, wonder and surprise

### Takeaway
Around 6–7, children enter the riddle and pun stage, enjoying double meanings and incongruities they can just barely "get". Jokes that match a newly acquired concept (such as conservation) are funniest. Violated expectations (surprise or "magic") measurably boost what children learn about the surprising thing immediately afterwards, at least in 3–6-year-olds.

### Cited Findings
**Humor**
- In McGhee's stage model, the "riddles and jokes (double meanings)" stage runs from about 6–7 to 10–11 years. It requires noticing a word or phrase with two meanings, which is incongruity resolution. Earlier stages rely on incongruous actions, naming and silly sounds. — summarized in [JCLAD paper](https://distantreader.org/stacks/journals/jclad/jclad-88.pdf); [Baker 2017 PhD thesis, Cardiff](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/105249/1/2017BakerGPhD.pdf)
  - Note: sources number the stages differently (Stage 5 vs. Stage 6).
- Riddles are reportedly funniest in grades 1–2 and become less funny as they become too easy. This is consistent with McGhee's cognitive-congruency principle: humor peaks at moderate challenge. — summary in [IEJEE article](https://iejee.com/index.php/IEJEE/article/download/225/221/452); McGhee 1976, *Child Development*, [doi:10.2307/1128797](https://doi.org/10.2307/1128797) †
- Ambiguity types differ in difficulty for children in UK Years 2, 4 and 6. Lexical and phonological ambiguities, such as homophone puns, are the easiest. Syntactic and idiomatic ones are harder. — [JCLAD paper](https://distantreader.org/stacks/journals/jclad/jclad-88.pdf) (proposal, n = 60)

**Surprise and violated expectations**
- 11-month-olds (N = 110) learned a hidden property of an object (a sound) only after that object violated solidity or continuity expectations. Learning was greater after violation, F(1,36) = 10.69, p = .002, partial η² = .23. The effect was specific to the violating object and did not transfer to a new object. — Stahl & Feigenson 2015, *Science* 348:91–94, [PMC5861377](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5861377/)
- Across four experiments, 3–6-year-olds learned novel words after a surprising event (an object seemed to change location "magically") but not after an expected one (the object was visibly moved). Again, the boost applied only to the surprising object. — Stahl & Feigenson 2017, *Cognition* 163:1–14, [doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2017.02.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2017.02.008); [abstract summary](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/stahl-feigenson-expectancyviolationspromotelearninginyoungchildren-2017)
- Curiosity is a state in which an information gap motivates seeking. In adults, curiosity enhances memory even for incidental material. — Kidd & Hayden 2015, *Neuron*, [doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.010) †; Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath 2014, *Neuron*, [doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2014.08.060](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2014.08.060) †

### Inferences
- The AI "knowing" the child's name and parents' jobs is itself an expectation violation. Under Stahl & Feigenson's findings, the moment right after that surprise is the best time to teach the one key idea ("I know because a grown-up told the computer… this is called AI"). The learning boost is object-specific, so attach the explanation directly to the surprising thing.
- Good humor for 6–8: homophone puns and simple riddles ("What do you call a sleeping dinosaur? A dino-*snore*!"), silly incongruity (the AI guesses the child's favourite food is "socks"), silly sounds and voice changes. For 7–8-year-olds, conservation-style "logic" jokes land. The AI should be the butt of any joke, never the child.
- Puns depend on language. Hindi/Hinglish riddles may land better than English puns for some children (see Section 7).

### Gaps
- The 2017 surprise study used 3–6-year-olds; there is no direct 7–8-year-old replication.
- No study was found on whether *conversational* surprises (an AI knowing personal facts) produce the same learning boost as physical-object violations.
- McGhee's 1979 primary text was not retrieved; the stage ages are via secondary sources.

## 7. Cross-cultural notes: Indian children, classroom norms, deference, Hindi/English/Hinglish

### Takeaway
Indian primary classrooms are, on average, teacher-centred and rote-heavy, and children are often expected to be quiet and obedient towards adults. Expect children to be brief and deferential until explicitly invited to talk, and to answer the way they would to a teacher. Hindi–English code-switching is common, but almost no child Hinglish speech data exist, which is a practical ASR risk.

### Cited Findings
- Indian teachers are often expected to be classroom disciplinarians, which pushes them toward authoritarian practices. — [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/examining-the-factors-shaping-pedagogical-practices-in-india)
- A UNESCO-based summary reports that teachers commonly aim to shape "quiet and obedient students who respect them", an idea linked to the traditional guru. — [Nordic J. Educational History via Umeå](https://journals.ub.umu.se/index.php/njedh/article/download/270/234/1333) (secondary; not independently verified)
- A study of 60 government primary teachers in 3 states found that most Indian classrooms remain dominated by rote learning despite decades of reform. — [BAICE PhD abstract](https://baice.ac.uk/phd-abstract/the-role-of-teachers-beliefs-in-the-implementation-of-learner-centred-education-in-india/)
- Clarke's ethnography argues that cultural beliefs shape Indian teachers' pedagogy in ways that are rarely examined. — Clarke 2001, *Teaching and Learning: The Culture of Pedagogy*, [publisher listing](https://www.schweitzer-online.de/en/buch/Clarke/Teaching-Learning/9780761995470/A3523838/)
- Hindi–English code-switching is common in Indian classrooms, especially in primary school, yet teachers often view it negatively, as "language corruption" acceptable only informally. — Pallavi 2016, *Journal of Indian Education* (NCERT), [link](https://ejournals.ncert.gov.in/index.php/jie/article/view/2139)
- Among North Indian bilingual adults, younger speakers use more English and tend to alternate whole English or Hindi clauses. Older speakers insert English words into Hindi clauses. — Klingler 2017, *Lifespans and Styles*, [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/5e61c48077c648aebb7c8d9888d8d3a2) (adult data)
- The HiACC Hinglish corpus includes children's speech recorded in a government primary school. The authors note that public code-switched speech datasets are scarce and that none before it included children. — [PMC12329218](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12329218/)
- India's National Education Policy 2020 recommends the home language or mother tongue as the medium of instruction until at least Grade 5. — [NEP 2020 (Ministry of Education)](https://www.education.gov.in/sites/upload_files/mhrd/files/NEP_Final_English_0.pdf) †
- The smart-speaker and robot-perception studies above sampled mostly White, middle-class Western children (e.g., 75% White in Flanagan et al. 2023). — [Duke summary](https://today.duke.edu/2023/04/kids-judge-alexa-smarter-roomba-say-both-deserve-kindness)

### Inferences
- Many Indian 6–8-year-olds will treat the AI like a teacher. Expect "Yes, ma'am/sir" style short answers, waiting to be asked, looking to the facilitator for permission, and possibly answering in a recitation register. Explicit permission ("There are no wrong answers!", "You can ask me anything!") and a facilitator nod should help. Open-ended invitations will need more modelling and patience than with Western samples.
- The character should accept English, Hindi or a mix, and mirror the child's language choice after their first utterance rather than forcing English. In English-medium private schools, children may still use Hindi words for family terms (Mummy, Papa, Nani) and emotion words. The AI should use the family terms the adult entered.
- Indian-English phonology plus child-speech variability plus code-switching is a worst-case combination for off-the-shelf ASR. Design the conversation so that misrecognition is cheap: choice questions, generous interpretation, no "I didn't understand" loops.
- Parents' jobs: in India, some job titles may be in Hindi or be informal (shopkeeper, driver, homemaker). Job descriptions in the script must be respectful and status-neutral. "Homemaker" or "works at home taking care of the family" should be described with equal enthusiasm.
- Western findings on how children perceive AI may not carry over to Indian children. Indian 6–8-year-olds may attribute more or less mind to AI; this is unknown.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that directly measures Indian primary children's question-asking, deference to adults, or shyness with strangers relative to other cultures.
- No study was found on Hinglish code-switching *patterns* in 6–8-year-olds, beyond the HiACC corpus description.
- No study was found of Indian children's perceptions of AI or voice assistants at 6–8.

## Design implications

Numbered rules derived from the findings above. Rules marked (I) are design inferences that go beyond the direct evidence; the rest follow closely from cited findings.

### A. How the AI talks
1. **One idea per sentence, one question per turn, question last.** Keep sentences to about 6–12 words with simple subject–verb–object structure. Speak at most 2–3 short sentences, about 5–8 seconds, before handing over the turn. Never stack two questions. (Working memory ≈2.5 chunks at grade 1, Cowan 2016; MLU ≈5.5–6 morphemes, Rice 2010) (I for exact numbers)
2. **One instruction at a time.** If the child must do something, make it a physical action ("wave at me!"), because enactment helps 7–9-year-olds follow instructions (Jaroslawska 2016).
3. **Use concrete words, no idioms or metaphors.** "Sharp as a tack", "my brain is made of math" and "the cloud" will be taken literally (Winner 1976). If you use an analogy, make it concrete and state the mapping explicitly (Holyoak 1984).
4. **Natural, unhurried pace with clear pauses between sentences; do not artificially slow down.** Slowing gives no benefit to typical children and faster speech hurts (Montgomery 2004; Abrahamse 2025). (I: about normal adult conversational rate, with about 300–500 ms gaps between sentences)
5. **Loudness: aim for the voice to be at least +15 dB, ideally about +20 dB, above room noise** (grade 1 needs +15.5 dB for 95% intelligibility; Bradley & Sato). Use an external speaker and a quiet corner; the laptop speaker alone is not enough in a busy hall.

### B. How the AI listens (timing)
6. **End-of-turn detection: tolerate about 1.5 s of silence inside a child's utterance before treating the turn as over.** Adult-tuned settings (about 0.5–0.7 s) will cut children off. (Children's longer latencies and greater speech variability: Casillas 2016; Lee et al. 1999) (I for the exact number)
7. **After a question, wait at least 3 s (Rowe's about 2.7–3 s threshold), and up to about 5–7 s, before re-prompting.** During the wait the character should look attentive (idle animation, "listening" pose), not blank. (I for the upper bound)
8. **Escalating re-prompt ladder:** (1) silent wait; (2) a gentle "Take your time…"; (3) rephrase as an easy two-option choice ("Cats or dogs?"); (4) offer a non-verbal option ("Show me a thumbs-up!"); (5) the facilitator steps in. Never just repeat the same question louder. (I, grounded in rapport, wait-time and repair research)
9. **Do not read gaze aversion as disengagement.** Children aged 5–8 look away while thinking (Doherty-Sneddon 2002). Use the webcam for presence, not for "attention" scoring.
10. **Interpret generously; ask for a repeat at most once.** Expect /r/, /l/, /th/ substitutions (/θ/ is mastered only by 6;0–6;11; Crowe & McLeod 2020), Indian-English phonology and code-switching. Children's own repair strategy is to repeat or speak louder (Cheng 2018), which does not fix recognition errors. On low confidence, move to a choice question.

### C. Warming up a shy child (first about 60 s)
11. **The AI greets first, by name, warmly, and asks nothing hard for the first 2–3 turns.** Start with a "talk-at" reveal plus a yes-or-wave opener ("Hi Aarav! Can you wave at me?"). Then move to easy two-option questions, then to an open "Tell me about…" invitation once the child is talking. (Behavioural inhibition in 15–20%; open-ended rapport benefits, Roberts 2004 and the 35-study meta-analysis) (I for the sequence)
12. **Always allow non-verbal participation** (wave, nod, thumbs-up, pointing at the screen) and never pressure ("Say something!"). Plan for about 1 child in 6 being markedly inhibited and occasional near-mute children (selective mutism about 0.7%).
13. **Facilitator position:** visible and close (beside, slightly behind the child), ready to model ("Wow, she knows your name! Tell her your favourite game!"). Indian children especially may look to the adult for permission. (I)
14. **Back-channel actively** ("Wow!", "Mm-hm!", "Really?") and reflect the child's words back ("A *red* bicycle!"). This trains narration and signals listening (NCAC narrative practice).
15. **Don't check understanding with yes/no questions** ("Do you understand?", "Is that OK?"). 5–9-year-olds answer yes/no questions even when they don't know (Waterman 2000). Use teach-back or two-option checks.

### D. Personalisation
16. **Use the child's first name at about 3–5 key moments:** greeting, the "reveal", a praise moment, the goodbye. Don't use it in every sentence. Own name evokes an enhanced attention response at about 8 years (Key et al.). (I for frequency)
17. **Make the child the expert.** Ask them to teach the AI about their family or their parent's job ("Your mummy is a nurse! What does a nurse do?"). This uses the self-reference effect (Cunningham 2013; Turk 2015) and personalization plus choice (Cordova & Lepper 1996), and gives the child competence. (I for the parent-job topic; direct evidence is thin)
18. **Reveal personal knowledge with its source in the same breath:** "Your teacher told me your name before you came, so I could say hello!" Children 6–8 understand informational access (Wimmer 1988). Older children expect personal facts from humans, not devices (Girouard-Hallam 2022). Stating the source turns "creepy" into "clever" and keeps the experience honest. (I for the "creepy" framing; untested)
19. **Vet the parent data** with the adult for sensitive situations (bereavement, separation, unemployment, "homemaker"), and describe all jobs with equal enthusiasm. (I)

### E. Praise and motivation
20. **Praise the process or the specific act, never the trait.** Say "You thought really hard!", "You told me so many details!", "That was a brave try!". Do not say "You're so smart / clever / a genius" (Kamins & Dweck 1999; Cimpian 2007; Zhao 2017). Expect modest effects; this is low-cost harm avoidance, not a mindset intervention (Li & Bates 2019; Sisk 2018).
21. **Keep praise sincere, specific and not inflated.** Avoid "That's the most incredible answer ever!" (Brummelman 2014; Henderlong & Lepper 2002). Never compare the child with other children.
22. **No contingent rewards** ("talk to me and you'll get a sticker"); tangible rewards undermine children's intrinsic motivation (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999; Lepper 1973). Unconditional souvenirs at the end are fine. (I)
23. **Offer 2–3 small choices** at least twice ("Joke or magic trick?", "Should I talk like a robot or like a mouse?") to support autonomy (SDT; Cordova & Lepper 1996).

### F. Keeping attention for 5 minutes
24. **Do not plan around "N minutes per year of age".** It has no primary evidence. Instead, structure the 5 minutes as about 5–6 beats of about 30–60 s, each with a change of activity or emotion: (1) greeting and name reveal, (2) easy choice game, (3) "teach me about your family" with the parent-job reveal, (4) joke or silly moment, (5) the "this is AI" explanation, (6) warm goodbye. Sustained attention at 6–8 is still developing rapidly and drops under high load (Betts 2006). (I for the beat structure)
25. **Keep the screen visually simple:** one character, minimal background motion, no text walls. Visual clutter increases off-task time in young children (Fisher 2014). Many 6-year-olds can't read fluently, so don't rely on on-screen text.
26. **Adapt by grade.** Class 1 children (about 6) get shorter turns, more choice questions and more non-verbal options. Class 3 children (about 8) can handle open "why/how" questions and riddles. Attention and working memory change fast across 5–9 (Betts 2006; Cowan 2016). (I)

### G. Humor, wonder, surprise
27. **Include at least one humor beat suited to the riddle/pun stage (6–7+):** a homophone pun or simple riddle, a silly voice, or the AI making an absurd guess about the child ("Is your favourite food… socks?"). Make the AI the butt of the joke, never the child. For Hindi-dominant children, prefer a Hindi riddle or silly sound over an English pun (McGhee's stages; ambiguity-difficulty data).
28. **Place the key teaching sentence immediately after the biggest surprise** (the name or parent reveal), and attach it to that surprising thing. Expectancy violations boost learning about the violating object in 3–6-year-olds (Stahl & Feigenson 2017).

### H. Explaining "this is AI"
29. **Use 3 short concrete sentences, a demo, and a contrast with magic.** For example: "I'm not magic. I'm a computer program called AI. A grown-up typed your name into the computer, and I learned how to talk by listening to lots and lots of people." Then do a tangible demo (e.g., "Say a word, and watch me turn it into a picture"). Children of 6–8 are moving from magical to physical explanations (Phelps & Woolley 1994), and preschoolers can grasp "computers learn from examples" (Williams 2019). (I for the script)
30. **Be honest about feelings and limits.** Children overestimate AI intelligence and are unsure about AI feelings (Andries & Robertson 2023), and younger children attribute more mind to Alexa (Flanagan 2023). Avoid "I love you" or "I'm your best friend forever". Prefer "I liked talking with you!" plus "I'm a computer, so I don't feel things the way you do."
31. **Check understanding with teach-back:** "Can you tell your teacher what AI is?" Don't ask "Did you understand?" (rule 15). Let the facilitator reinforce the message after the session.

### I. Language and culture (India)
32. **Mirror the child's language.** Start in the language the adult flagged (or a simple bilingual greeting), then follow the child's choice: English, Hindi or Hinglish. Use the family terms entered (Mummy/Papa/Nani). Code-switching is the norm, and NEP 2020 favours home language in early grades. (I)
33. **Give explicit permission to talk and to be wrong** ("There's no wrong answer!", "You can ask me anything!"). Expect teacher-style deference (short answers, "Yes ma'am"), so lean on choice questions and the facilitator's modelling. (Classroom-norm literature; I)
34. **Test on real Indian 6–8-year-olds before launch**, especially recognition of child Hinglish speech (public child code-switched data are almost non-existent; HiACC) and timing thresholds. Most of the AI-perception evidence above comes from Western samples. (I)

### Quick-reference parameters (all I, derived from the above)

| Parameter | Suggested starting value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| AI sentence length | 6–12 words, simple syntax | Child MLU about 5.5–6 morphemes; working memory about 2.5 chunks |
| AI turn length | 2–3 sentences, ≤ about 8 s, one question at the end | Working memory; one idea per utterance |
| Mid-utterance silence before closing child's turn | about 1.5 s | Children's longer latencies and pauses |
| Wait after a question before first re-prompt | 3–5 s (up to 7 s for shy children) | Rowe wait-time threshold about 2.7–3 s |
| Re-asks per recognition failure | at most 1, then switch to a choice question | Repair research; ASR risk |
| Uses of child's name | about 3–5 per session | Own-name attention capture |
| Activity beats in 5 min | about 5–6 beats of 30–60 s | Sustained attention still developing; no evidence for "min per year" rule |
| Voice level over room noise | at least +15 dB, ideally about +20 dB | Bradley & Sato grade-1 data |
| Choices offered | 2–3 options, at least twice | SDT; Cordova & Lepper |
